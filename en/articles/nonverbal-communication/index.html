Nonverbal communication: gestures, pointing and joint attention

A child who extends his arms to ask for a lap is communicating. The same happens when he points at a toy, hands an object to an adult or shakes his head to refuse something.

By the Talk+ team 17 October 2026 5 min read

A child who extends his arms to ask for a lap is communicating. The same happens when he points at a toy, hands an object to an adult or shakes his head to refuse something.

Long before producing sentences, children can transmit messages through their body, gaze, movements and facial expressions.

Knowing these forms of communication helps families recognize initiatives that sometimes go unnoticed.

What is nonverbal communication?

Nonverbal communication includes behaviors used to transmit information without necessarily using words.

Among the most common examples are:

  • Point to objects or events.
  • Extend your arms.
  • Say goodbye with your hand.
  • Shake your head.
  • Show or hand over objects.
  • Use facial expressions.
  • Orient the body towards a person or object.

The interpretation of these behaviors depends on the context and characteristics of the child.

A movement may have communicative intent in one situation and perform another function in a different situation.

Why is it important to point out?

Pointing can have different objectives.

A child may point to a toy because he intends to receive it. You can also point to a plane to share something that caught your attention with someone else.

In the first case, the gesture is associated with a request. In the second, it may represent an attempt to share an experience.

Both are important forms of communication.

However, not all children use the pointing gesture in the same way. Some resort to other movements, objects, vocalizations or communication devices.

What is joint attention?

Joint attention happens when two people share attention in relation to an object, event or activity.

Imagine a child who observes a dog, looks at the adult and returns his attention to the animal.

You may be looking to share an interest in what is happening.

Joint attention is associated with important learning processes and social and communicative development.

However, it should not be confused with the obligation to maintain eye contact. Some children, including autistic children, can share attention and interests without looking directly into another person's eyes.

The objective is to create a shared experience, respecting different forms of interaction.

What does it mean to have communicative intent?

Communicative intent exists when a behavior is used to convey a message to another person.

For example, a child may hand a closed container to an adult because he or she needs help opening it.

You can also move an object away to show that you don't want it.

When the adult recognizes and responds to these initiatives, it helps the child to realize that communication can produce results and allow participation in interactions.

Five activities to support nonverbal communication

1. Play with choices

Present two toys and allow the child to choose.

Accept responses through gestures, pointing, words, gaze or other accessible means.

2. Share picture books

Observe the images and comment on elements that spark the child’s interest.

You can point to a picture and say its name, without requiring the child to do the same.

3. Play in turns

Simple activities with balls, blocks or cars create opportunities to develop reciprocal interactions.

The adult participates, observes the child's reaction and adapts the game.

4. Use songs with gestures

Music accompanied by movements can associate actions, rhythm and language.

The child can participate through gestures, movements, sounds or facial expressions.

5. Create opportunities to ask for help

When a difficulty naturally arises, like a box that won't open, observe whether the child tries to communicate.

Offer support and model the expression “help,” an appropriate gesture or card.

It is not necessary to deliberately create frustrations or impede access to essential needs to provoke requests.

What about when the gestures are infrequent?

Some children present differences in the development of gestures, joint attention or communicative intention.

These differences may have different explanations and do not allow, in isolation, to conclude that there is a specific disturbance.

When there are persistent concerns, significant difficulties in communication or loss of previously acquired skills, it is important to seek professional assessment.

    Conclusion

    Communication does not begin when the first word appears.

    Gestures, movements, expressions and different ways of sharing interests constitute important components of communicative experiences.

    Recognizing these signs allows adults to respond more sensitively and create opportunities for language development.

    Before asking how many words a child says, it is worth observing how many ways they can already communicate.

    Informative content. It does not replace an individual assessment or guidance from a healthcare professional.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is children's nonverbal communication?

    It includes gestures, movements, expressions, gaze, body orientation, sounds and other ways of conveying messages without relying on words.

    Why is it important for the child to point?

    Pointing can be used to ask or to share interest. Some children use other equally valid ways to communicate these intentions.

    Does joint attention mean eye contact?

    No. It means sharing attention about something; it can happen without direct or constant eye contact.

    When do infrequent gestures warrant evaluation?

    When there are persistent concerns, significant impact on communication or loss of skills, it is important to seek professional assessment.

    This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
