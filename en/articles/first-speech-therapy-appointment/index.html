What happens at a first speech and language therapy appointment?

By the Talk+ team 15 October 2026 5 min read

Booking a first appointment can raise questions: will the child have to answer questions? Will they play? Can a family member stay? A first speech and language therapy appointment is an opportunity to understand the concerns, get to know the child and decide what assessment or next steps may help. The format varies by age, needs and service.

> In brief: there may be a conversation about development and concerns, observation of communication and time for questions. First appointments do not all have the same structure or activities; check details with the service.

What might the therapist ask?

The therapist may ask about the child’s development, health, hearing, languages, communication experiences, speech, language and participation at home or school. They may want to know what is already easy, what is difficult and what prompted the family to seek support.

You do not need perfect answers or technical terms. Concrete examples are useful: “They follow a request to bring their shoes but do not usually answer with words” describes an observation without deciding why it happens. If you do not know an answer, it is fine to say so.

Will the child be tested or expected to speak?

An assessment may include structured activities, conversation, play, books, pictures or objects. The approach depends on what the clinician needs to understand and how the child takes part. It should adapt to the child’s age, comfort, attention and communication methods.

A child may speak less in a new setting, need time or not want to interact. One appointment does not necessarily show everything they do at home or elsewhere. Family observations can add that context. Gestures, pointing, pictures, writing or AAC may also be ways to take part; an appointment should not depend on spoken responses.

How can family prepare?

  1. Write down your main questions. For example: “What is being assessed?”, “What may happen next?” and “How can we support communication while we wait?”
  2. Think of everyday examples. Consider how the child asks, chooses, refuses, comments, follows instructions or interacts with others.
  3. Describe the languages the child uses. Explain who speaks each language and in which situations; there is no need to stop using the family language.
  4. Bring relevant reports if available. Ask the service what is useful and how to share documents securely.
  5. Ask before recording or sharing videos. A home recording may be useful in some cases, but check whether it is needed and how it will be protected.
  6. Mention strengths and interests too. What the child enjoys and how they already communicate help build a fuller picture.

If the child uses AAC or another communication resource, bring it or tell the clinician. Do not remove it to “see whether they speak”; access to communication should remain available.

What may happen after the appointment?

Depending on the information available, the therapist may explain observations, suggest further assessment, offer general strategies, request information from another setting or recommend working with another professional. Hearing or other developmental questions may also need to be considered. A diagnosis or complete plan does not always follow from the first appointment.

Services differ in referral routes, format and availability. Families should check local arrangements or ask a doctor or paediatrician how to access care.

How can family take part without turning it into a test?

You can explain simply where you are going and who you will meet. Bring a familiar toy if the service allows it, keep the child’s communication tools available and avoid rehearsing answers on the way in. If the child needs a break, tell the clinician.

The appointment is also a place for the family’s questions. You can ask how goals will be chosen, what role family may have, how information could be shared with school and how to tell whether a strategy is still suitable. It is reasonable to ask for plain explanations and to say if a suggestion does not fit everyday life.

    Conclusion

    A first appointment is a chance to describe what the child already communicates, put concerns in context and discuss next steps. The child does not need to rehearse or arrive with a diagnosis. Asking the service how to prepare and bringing everyday examples can make the meeting more useful.

    > This article is for information and does not replace an individual assessment. Procedures vary between services.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does family stay in the room at the first appointment?

    It depends on the service, the child’s age and the session’s purpose. Ask in advance and tell the clinician what helps the child take part.

    Does a child have to speak to be assessed?

    Not necessarily. Depending on the clinical question, assessment can consider gestures, understanding, interaction, pictures and other communication methods.

    Should I bring toys or reports?

    Check with the service. Relevant previous reports may help; a familiar object may help some children feel comfortable.

    Will the first appointment give a diagnosis?

    Not always. It may be one step in gathering information and deciding what further assessment or support is appropriate.

    Should we stop speaking another language at home?

    No. Tell the clinician about the child’s languages and settings so the full language profile can be considered.

    This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
