Sustained attention games for children

Sustained attention games invite children to remain involved in a task for a while. Sitting still or looking at an adult is not the only way to show attention.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • A clear, accessible task can support involvement in that setting.
  • Playing longer does not prove a general attention improvement or rule out ADHD.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Environment, understanding, interest and the child’s current state all affect attention. NHS children’s services advise reducing noise, gaining attention before instructions and choosing accessible activities. A confusing game places demands on more than concentration.

Staying involved and waiting for a signal are different demands. They can be combined, but change one feature at a time to understand where support helps. The examples below are play suggestions; they do not establish transfer to every school task or constitute ADHD treatment.

References for this background: Norfolk and Waveney Children’s Health Services — Attention & Listening · Children and Family Health Devon — Supporting listening skills: activities to try.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • A mission with an endingChoose three large objects to place in a scene. Demonstrate their destinations and complete one mission at a time. The ending is a finished scene, not enduring a timer.
  • A search trailPlace familiar drawings along a short route and find them one by one. A child can move, use mobility support or point from a comfortable position.
  • Ready, steady, goRoll a soft ball after an agreed signal. Demonstrate and keep the wait short. Let the child give the signal after several turns. Nobody is eliminated for moving early.

Making the activity accessible

Remove unnecessary distractions, explain one rule and agree a break. Reduce objects or steps if the child loses track. Movement and alternative responses can support participation. Do not withhold basic needs, demand eye contact or turn play into an obedience test.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Discuss persistent difficulties affecting several settings with school and health professionals. Share examples, supports tried and the effects of sleep, noise or task complexity. A short round or an off day cannot establish a diagnosis.

What we can conclude

What we can say

  • A clear, accessible task can support involvement in that setting.

What we cannot say

  • Playing longer does not prove a general attention improvement or rule out ADHD.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a child concentrate?

There is no simple minutes-per-age rule that assesses an individual child. Context, task and support matter.

Must the child sit still?

No. Movement and adapted access can be compatible with engagement.

Can games treat ADHD?

These suggestions do not replace assessment, school support or recommended care.

What if the child wants to stop?

Respect refusal, explore discomfort and adjust or end the activity.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
