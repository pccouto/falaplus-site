Does tongue-tie delay speech? What the evidence says

When a child talks little or mixes up sounds, it is common to hear that they are «tongue-tied» and that «snipping the tie» will fix it. The scientific evidence is far more cautious.

By the Talk+ team 24 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Tongue-tie, or ankyloglossia, is a membrane under the tongue that is shorter or thicker than usual. In this article we explain what is known about tongue-tie and speech, what reviews say about frenotomy and what to check first when there is a delay.

In short

  • A 2020 consensus of paediatric ENT surgeons states that ankyloglossia does not typically affect speech.
  • A tongue-tie does not explain a language delay: it does not reduce vocabulary or sentence building.
  • Reviews of frenotomy to improve speech find insufficient, low-quality evidence.
  • Before surgery for speech reasons, an assessment by a speech and language therapist is recommended.

Does tongue-tie delay speech?

It is important to separate two things. Language is knowing words and putting them into sentences; speech is producing the sounds (see Speech, language and communication). A tongue-tie may, in some cases, make sounds that need the tongue tip raised harder, but it does not explain a child who says few words.

A consensus statement by paediatric otolaryngologists published in 2020 concluded that ankyloglossia does not typically affect speech, and recommends that an older child having a frenotomy for speech reasons is first assessed by a speech and language therapist (Messner et al., 2020).

Does cutting the tongue-tie improve speech?

A systematic review of ankyloglossia treatment for reasons other than breastfeeding found few studies: two randomised trials, two cohort studies and eleven case series. Two cohort studies reported improvements in articulation, but outcome assessment was inconsistent and the evidence was judged insufficient (Chinnadurai et al., 2015). More recent reviews reach similar conclusions.

What we know

  • Most children with a short frenulum speak without difficulty.
  • Some sounds (such as l, r, t, d) may need more tongue movement.
  • Decisions about surgery should involve a doctor and a speech and language therapist.

What we cannot say

  • «A tongue-tie explains why the child isn’t talking.»
  • «Cutting the tie will get the child talking.»
  • «All sound errors come from the tongue-tie.»

What to check first when there is a speech delay

  • Hearing: the first step for any delay (see Glue ear and speech).
  • Language and communication: understanding, vocabulary, gestures and interaction.
  • Speech sounds: whether sound errors are typical for the child’s age (see Speech sound errors in children).
  • Tongue function: movement, eating and swallowing, with the doctor and therapist.

It is also wise to be wary of blowing or facial exercises sold as a solution for speech: see do facial exercises speed up speech?.

Frequently asked questions about tongue-tie and speech

Does tongue-tie delay speech?

Rarely. A tongue-tie does not explain a child saying few words; in some cases it may make certain sounds harder.

How do I know if my child is tongue-tied?

Only a professional can confirm it. Signs such as difficulty sticking the tongue out or lifting it deserve an assessment.

Does cutting a tongue-tie help speech?

The evidence is insufficient. Before surgery for speech reasons, an assessment by a speech and language therapist is recommended.

Can tongue-tie cause a lisp?

A lisp has many possible causes and is common in young children. An assessment can tell whether tongue movement plays a part.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
