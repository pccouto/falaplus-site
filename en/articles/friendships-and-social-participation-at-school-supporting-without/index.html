Friendships and social participation at school: supporting without forcing

Social participation is important, but it cannot be measured solely by the number of friends, eye contact or frequency of conversations. Some children prefer brief interactions, shared interests, or alone time. Support must open up opportunities and respect consent and preferences.

Social participation is important, but it cannot be measured solely by the number of friends, eye contact or frequency of conversations. Some children prefer brief interactions, shared interests, or alone time. Support must open up opportunities and respect consent and preferences.

Create opportunities with common interests

Pair or small group activities around an interest can facilitate interaction. Adults can model ways to invite, wait, refuse, and repair misunderstandings without imposing physical contact or participation. The child can observe before entering or choose another activity.

Support colleagues and prevent exclusion

The class can learn that people communicate and play in different ways, without exposing a student's private information. Adult mediation helps resolve conflicts and distribute roles in a meaningful way. Check that the child feels safe and that the adaptations increase choice and belonging.

In short

  • Social participation takes different forms and must respect preferences.
  • The objective is to expand opportunities, not normalize interaction.

In summary

The school can create conditions for reciprocal relationships without turning friendship into an obligatory goal.

Frequently asked questions

Does this theme apply the same to all children?

No. The decision must consider development, preferences, context and individual assessment.

When should I ask for help?

When the difficulty persists, affects communication, learning, safety or well-being, talk to qualified professionals and the school, depending on the context.

Does this information replace an individual assessment?

No. This article is general information. Decisions should be based on the child’s needs, context and assessment by qualified professionals.

Where can families find reliable guidance?

Start with the official sources linked in this article and discuss the child’s situation with the school and relevant qualified professionals.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
