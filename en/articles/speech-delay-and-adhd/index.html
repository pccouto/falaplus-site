Speech delay and ADHD: how are they connected?

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and language difficulties occur together more often than people think. And when language is left out of the assessment, the child may miss the support they need.

By the Talk+ team 24 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Many families ask whether ADHD causes speech delay. The short answer is that they are different conditions that often overlap. In this article we explain what studies show about ADHD and language, why both should be assessed and how to support the child every day.

In short

  • In a study of 6- to 8-year-olds, 40% of children with ADHD had language problems, compared with 17% of other children.
  • A meta-analysis of 21 studies found differences in expressive, receptive and pragmatic language.
  • Language problems were linked to poorer school performance, regardless of ADHD symptoms.
  • A child with ADHD should have their language assessed; a child with a language delay may have explanations other than attention.

Does ADHD cause speech delay?

ADHD is not a language disorder, but the two often coexist. In an Australian study of 179 children with ADHD and 212 without, aged 6 to 8, 40% of children with ADHD had language problems, compared with 17% of the comparison group (Sciberras et al., 2014).

A meta-analysis of 21 studies, with 1,209 children with ADHD and 1,101 without, found lower performance in expressive, receptive and pragmatic language (the social use of language) (Korrel et al., 2017). The authors caution that part of these differences may reflect broader difficulties, not only language ones.

  • Expressive languageOrganising sentences, finding words, telling a story with a beginning, middle and end.
  • Receptive languageUnderstanding long instructions or instructions with several parts.
  • PragmaticsTaking turns to talk, staying on topic, adjusting to the listener.

Why does this matter at school?

In the Sciberras study, language problems were linked to poorer academic functioning, even after accounting for ADHD symptom severity and other conditions. Yet few of these children were seeing a speech and language therapist.

In practice, a child who «doesn’t listen» to instructions may be inattentive, may not have understood the sentence, or both. Only a language assessment can tell.

What we know

  • Language problems are common in children with ADHD.
  • They affect expression, comprehension and the social use of language.
  • They are linked to poorer school performance.

What we cannot conclude

  • That a speech delay at age 2 is a sign of ADHD.
  • That treating attention will fix language on its own.
  • That every child with ADHD has language problems.

What to do: assessment and support

  1. If the child has ADHD, ask for their language to be assessed too by a speech and language therapist.
  2. If the child has a language delay, remember that ADHD is not usually diagnosed before school age; other causes, such as hearing, should be checked first.
  3. Give short instructions, one at a time, and check the child has understood.
  4. Use visual supports (pictures, sequences) for tasks with several steps.
  5. In conversation, help them organise what happened: «first…», «then…», «in the end…».

Frequently asked questions about ADHD and language

Can ADHD cause speech delay?

They are different conditions that often occur together. In one study of 6- to 8-year-olds, about 40% of children with ADHD had language problems.

Do children with ADHD talk later?

Some do, but talking late is not a sign of ADHD. A speech delay should be assessed in its own right, starting with hearing.

How can I tell inattention from a comprehension problem?

Only a language assessment can tell. Instructions the child does not follow may not have been understood.

Does speech therapy help children with ADHD?

When there are language difficulties, yes, it can help. What matters is that those difficulties are identified.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
