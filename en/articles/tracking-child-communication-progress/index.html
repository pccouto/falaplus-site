How to track small steps in a child’s communication

Progress in communication does not always appear first as a new spoken word. A child may start a game, choose between options, use a picture, understand a routine or show that they want to stop. By the Talk+ team 15 October 2026 5 min read

Progress in communication does not always appear first as a new spoken word. A child may start a game, choose between options, use a picture, understand a routine or show that they want to stop. Tracking a child’s communication progress means noticing changes in everyday life, without comparing them with other children or turning family notes into a clinical scale.

> In brief: record concrete examples, the context and the communication method. Gestures, pointing, understanding, choices, initiation and participation matter too. A checklist can help recall observations; it does not diagnose or measure development in a standardised way.

What might be useful to notice?

A helpful note describes what happened rather than claiming to know the child’s intention. For example, “at snack time, pointed to the banana after being shown two options” is more observable than “now knows how to ask for a banana”. You might note whether the child:

  • starts an interaction or gets someone’s attention;
  • makes a choice, request, refusal or indicates they are finished;
  • uses speech, vocalisations, gestures, pictures, signs or writing;
  • understands words or instructions in familiar routines;
  • combines methods, such as pointing and vocalising, to convey a message;
  • takes part with more people or in different settings;
  • uses a communication support more independently or in a new way;
  • shows a preference, discomfort or need for a break.

These observations do not prove a skill is present in every situation. A child may gesture at home but not at school, or communicate more when rested. Variation between settings is useful information, not failure.

How can you make a simple record?

You can use a phone note or sheet of paper without collecting more information than needed. Record four things:

What to noteExample
SituationSnack time, at home, with two people present
What happenedPointed to the banana when offered two options
Communication methodPointing and vocalisation
Support or responseAdult named the choice and handed over the banana

Add a date only if it helps you remember changes. You do not need to record every interaction or count words every day. A few representative examples may be enough for a conversation with the child’s support team.

Observation checklist — no score

You may mark “observed”, “not observed yet” or “not sure” to help remember examples. Do not add up answers or interpret them as a level, delay or diagnosis.

  • Does the child start any interaction with another person?
  • Can they show choices or preferences in some way?
  • Do they have an accessible way to say “no”, “stop” or “finished”?
  • Do they understand familiar words or instructions in routines?
  • Do they use gestures, pictures, signs, writing or speech to ask for help?
  • Do they take part in a shared activity in a way they prefer?
  • Does communication vary with the person, place, language, noise or tiredness?
  • What changes seem to make participation easier?

“Not observed yet” does not mean the child lacks a skill. It may not have come up, or the question may not have been suitable. Do not prompt responses just to complete the checklist.

How can you share observations with professionals?

Choose a few examples that show what concerns you and what is going well. Ask the speech and language therapist, paediatrician or educator what information would help. If the child uses AAC, describe where it is available and which messages they can communicate; do not remove the system to test spoken communication.

Photos, videos and records can contain personal information. Before sending them, check whether the service needs them, which channel to use and who will have access. Avoid recording intimate details or sharing them publicly. A written description may be enough.

What should you not conclude from these records?

A family record cannot determine whether a child has a disorder, is “on track” or improved because of one strategy. More words used in a game, for example, do not show that the child uses them spontaneously elsewhere. Clinical interpretation requires context and professional assessment.

Do not compare siblings, classmates or children of different ages. Notes can support a conversation and help identify meaningful goals, but do not replace validated tools or professional observation.

    Conclusion

    Small everyday examples can help families and professionals discuss communication more concretely. Recording different communication methods and the context gives a richer picture than counting words alone. Use notes to support discussion, never to diagnose or rank a child.

    Frequently asked questions

    What counts as progress in communication?

    It may include more initiation, choices, understanding, participation or use of different methods, not only new spoken words.

    Should I count how many words a child says each day?

    That may not reflect understanding, gestures, AAC or use across settings. Ask a professional what information is relevant to your concern.

    Can a checklist tell me whether there is a delay?

    No. This checklist is only for organising examples; it is not standardised or diagnostic.

    Is the app report an assessment?

    No. It summarises use of Talk+ and does not assess communication or development.

    How can I record communication through gestures or pictures?

    Describe the situation, what the child did and how others responded, without assuming meaning when it is unclear.
