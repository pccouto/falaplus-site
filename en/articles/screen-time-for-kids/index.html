Screen time for kids: how much, how and with whom

Screens: yes or no? The more useful question is: what content, how long, with whom and instead of what. See the guidelines by age and how to choose a good educational app.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 4 min read
Loading reads…

Screen time for kids worries many families and educators. In this guide we bring together the health guidelines, what research says about screens and development, and practical criteria for choosing educational apps for kids.

In short

  • In the early years, guidelines point to little or no sedentary screen time.
  • What matters most is the content, an adult’s presence and what the screen replaces.
  • A digital game can complement, but does not replace free play or conversation.
  • A good app has no ads, respects the child’s pace and promises no health outcomes.

How much screen time is recommended by age?

The World Health Organization (2019) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (2016) have published guidance for the early years:

AgeWorld Health OrganizationAmerican Academy of Pediatrics
Under 1 yearscreen time not recommendedavoid screens (except video calls) until 18 months
1 yearsedentary screen time not recommendedfrom 18 to 24 months, only high-quality content with an adult
2 to 4 yearsno more than 1 hour a day; less is betterfrom 2 to 5 years, up to 1 hour a day of high-quality content, ideally watched together

These are general guidelines. The child’s doctor or paediatrician can help adapt them to each child and family.

What does research say about screens?

The WHO and AAP guidance stresses that the effect of screens depends on several factors, not just the minutes:

  • ContentCalm, age-appropriate content without ads is not the same as fast, endless videos.
  • With whomAn adult playing and talking alongside turns the screen into a reason to communicate.
  • Instead of whatThe bigger problem is when screens take the place of sleep, movement, family meals and conversation.
  • WhenScreens before bed can disrupt sleep.

It also matters not to blame a language difficulty on a single cause: if you are worried about speech, see Speech delay. And remember that a digital game, even an interactive one, is not the same as free play with objects and people: see The importance of play.

How to choose an educational app for kids

  1. A clear, honest goal. Be wary of promises like «develops the brain» or «gets your child talking».
  2. No ads aimed at children and no hidden purchases.
  3. The right pace: instructions that can be heard again and time to think.
  4. Difficulty that adapts: neither too easy nor too hard.
  5. Positive feedback without punishment: a mistake is a chance to try again.
  6. Accessible and calm: few stimuli, adjustable sound, simple controls.
  7. Privacy: a clear policy and protected adult settings.

Simple everyday rules

  • Play alongside, especially at first: name, comment and celebrate.
  • Link it to the real world: if the game showed an apple, look for an apple in the kitchen.
  • Agree on start and end rules, with a warning before it ends.
  • No screens at mealtimes or in the hour before bed.
  • Watch the child: are they enjoying it and learning, or just tapping?

Frequently asked questions about screen time

How much screen time can a 2-year-old have?

The WHO recommends, from 2 to 4 years, no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day, and says less is better. The AAP suggests high-quality content, ideally watched with an adult.

Do screens cause speech delay?

A difficulty should not be blamed on a single cause. What matters most is the time, the content, an adult’s presence and whether the screen takes the place of conversation, sleep and play.

Do educational apps help children learn?

A good app can give practice at specific tasks and be a reason to talk. Language is learned mainly through interaction with people.

Do video calls with grandparents count?

The AAP makes an exception for video calls in its guidance for babies: they are a form of interaction with people.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
