Lisp in children: speaking with the tongue between the teeth

Instead of “sun”, the child says something like “thun”, with the tip of the tongue peeking between the teeth. A lisp is very common in the early years and often fades on its own. In other cases, it is worth getting help.

A lisp is a difference in producing sibilant sounds, mainly /s/ and /z/, and sometimes “sh” and the sound in “measure”. In this article we explain the types of lisp, until what age it is expected and what can help.

In short

  • An interdental lisp (tongue between the teeth) is common and can be expected until about age 4–5.
  • A lateral lisp (air escapes at the sides, with a “slushy” sound) is not part of typical development.
  • Teeth, mouth breathing and prolonged pacifier use can contribute.
  • A speech and language therapist assesses and, if needed, works directly on the sound, not with blowing exercises.

Types of lisp

  • InterdentalThe tip of the tongue sits between the front teeth. It is the most common type, especially in young children.
  • DentalisedThe tongue pushes against the back of the teeth; the sound is muffled.
  • LateralAir escapes over the sides of the tongue, with a “wet” or slushy sound. It is not typical at any age.
  • PalatalThe tongue touches the roof of the mouth further back than it should.

Until what age is it normal?

Sibilant sounds are among the slowest to settle. A study pooling data from 27 languages found that most consonants were acquired by around age 5 (McLeod and Crowe, 2018). An interdental lisp at 3 or 4 is generally expected; if it persists after 5, or bothers the child, an assessment makes sense.

A lateral lisp is different: because it does not usually go away on its own, it can be assessed earlier. See also Speech sound errors.

What can contribute?

  • Losing front baby teeth, which leaves a temporary gap.
  • An open bite or other dental differences.
  • Mouth breathing, for example with enlarged adenoids or allergies.
  • Prolonged pacifier use or thumb sucking (see Does a pacifier delay speech?).
  • In many cases there is no identifiable cause.

What helps at home?

  • Do not correct constantly or ask for repetition: model the right sound calmly (“Yes, it’s the sun!”).
  • Talk face to face, so the child can see the position of your lips and teeth.
  • Play with the snake sound (“sssss”), teeth together and tongue tucked away.
  • If there is mouth breathing or snoring, talk to a doctor.

What can be said

  • An interdental lisp is common in the preschool years.
  • A lateral lisp deserves an earlier assessment.
  • Therapy works directly on producing the sound.

What cannot be said

  • “A lisp is always caused by tongue-tie.”
  • “Blowing exercises fix a lisp.”
  • “At 7 it is too late to help.”

Frequently asked questions about lisps

Until what age is a lisp normal?

An interdental lisp is common until about age 4–5. If it continues after that, or if it is lateral, an assessment is worthwhile.

Does a lisp go away on its own?

An interdental lisp often does. A lateral lisp rarely disappears without support.

Does tongue-tie cause a lisp?

It is rarely the only explanation. See the article on tongue-tie and speech.

How is a lisp treated?

A speech and language therapist teaches the tongue position and practises the sound in syllables, words and sentences, step by step.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
