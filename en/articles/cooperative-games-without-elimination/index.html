Cooperative games that include every child

Cooperative games for children offer a shared task, such as building a bridge or getting characters home. The aim is shared decision-making rather than turning waiting into an endurance test.

Cooperative games for children offer a shared task, such as building a bridge or getting characters home. The aim is shared decision-making rather than turning waiting into an endurance test.

Cooperative games for children: what is known?

NAEYC presents games and shared activities as opportunities to practise turns, cooperation and handling frustration with support. These are educational possibilities, not evidence that any game changes social participation beyond play. (NAEYC)

Developmentally appropriate practice includes agency and individual support. Communication can use several modalities. Participation should therefore not depend on fast speech, tolerating discomfort or making the same movement as everyone else. (NAEYC) (ASHA)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Choose a visible shared goal, such as building a road for three vehicles. Begin with two people and a few materials.
  2. Offer possible actions: choose a piece, place it, hold a base or indicate a destination. Choosing and commenting can also be turns.
  3. Demonstrate a simple sequence: “you choose, I place”. Avoid a long explanation of rules before play.
  4. Keep turns short and offer an action during waiting if useful. Never remove a communication system to force device sharing.
  5. Review the result together and allow an ending or change of plan. Do not eliminate a child for mistakes or make continued play a reward for enduring distress.

What might this look like in a real situation?

During a mission to bring animals to a shelter, one child chooses a character, another moves the bridge and an adult stabilises it. If the bridge falls, the group decides how to rebuild. The error belongs to the shared problem, not to a child blamed for “ruining everything”.

How can access be adapted?

In preschool, a brief exchange may be enough; extend it through interest rather than a rigid age rule. A child may prefer parallel play before cooperation. Explain the beginning and end, accept breaks and make “no”, “help” and “another idea” accessible.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Notice reciprocal choices, room to refuse and roles with actual influence. Sitting nearby does not establish participation. If one child always holds materials while others decide, review the roles. Do not score friendliness, eye contact or hiding discomfort.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • A shared goal may create opportunities for joint decisions and turns.
  • Adapting rules and actions may allow more children to participate.

What we cannot say

  • Cooperative games treat autism, ADHD or social difficulties.
  • Cooperation means always accepting others’ choices and never leaving.

Frequently asked questions

How can a child learn to wait for a turn?

Start with brief, predictable exchanges and support during waiting. Adjust the demand rather than measuring endurance alone.

What if a child does not want group play?

Respect refusal, investigate barriers and offer another way to approach. Parallel play can also be a valid choice.

Do cooperative games need to be bought?

No. Build a road or organise a mission with safe objects. Shared decisions and goals matter more than a commercial game.

Must children speak to have a turn?

No. A gesture, AAC selection or action can contribute. Agree on an accessible response method.
