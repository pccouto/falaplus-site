Speech delay: the child understands everything but does not talk

“They understand everything we say, fetch their shoes, point to what they want… they just don’t talk.” It is one of the most common descriptions of a speech delay. Good understanding is a favourable sign, but it is no reason to skip an assessment.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

When a child understands everything but does not talk, professionals speak of a delay mainly in expressive language. In this article we explain why understanding matters so much, which causes still need considering and how to help the child take the step to words. See also Receptive language and Speech delay.

In short

  • Among late talkers, those who understand well generally do better.
  • Even so, hearing and how the child produces sounds need assessing.
  • A motor speech difficulty, such as apraxia, can occur with good understanding.
  • Gestures, pictures and choices give the child ways to communicate while words arrive.

Why is good understanding a good sign?

A review of late talkers concluded that children with an expressive-only delay tend to have a better outlook than those who also have difficulties understanding, although no single factor predicts progress with certainty (Rescorla, 2011). The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association lists understanding, gestures and imitation among the factors to assess (ASHA, Late Language Emergence).

What still needs assessing?

  • HearingA child can understand from context and still hear some sounds poorly. See Hearing tests.
  • Sound productionIf the child uses very few sounds, finds imitation hard or errors vary a lot, there may be a motor difficulty. See Childhood apraxia of speech.
  • Real understandingSometimes the child follows routine and gestures rather than words. The therapist assesses this carefully.
  • InteractionEye contact, shared interest and play help build the full picture.

How to help words come

  • Start with fun sounds: animal and vehicle noises (“boom!”, “woof”, “vroom”), which are easier than words.
  • Accept and name gestures: if they point to water, say “Water! You want water.”
  • Offer a choice between two things, saying the names slowly.
  • Wait expectantly without demanding: an “I’m waiting” look works better than “say water”.
  • Offer alternative ways to communicate, such as signs or pictures (see Does AAC stop speech?).
  • Use the 8 strategies for home in daily routines.

Frequently asked questions: understands everything but does not talk

The child understands everything but does not talk. Should I worry?

Good understanding is a favourable sign, but the speech delay should be assessed, including hearing.

Why doesn’t the child talk if they understand?

It may be an expressive language delay, a motor difficulty producing sounds or other causes. Only an assessment can tell.

Does using gestures delay speech?

No. Gestures and pictures support communication and do not stop speech.

How long should I wait?

If the child is 2 or older and says very few words, there is no need to wait before asking for an assessment.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
