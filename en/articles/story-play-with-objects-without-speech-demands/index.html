Story play with objects, without speech demands

Story play with objects gives characters and actions a concrete presence. A box, a large figure and a cloth can start a story even when a child does not yet tell one in words.

Story play with objects gives characters and actions a concrete presence. A box, a large figure and a cloth can start a story even when a child does not yet tell one in words.

Story play with objects: what is known?

NAEYC describes guided play as a context where adults prepare materials and offer suggestions while children retain agency. Adults can introduce a story problem without prescribing every action and response. (NAEYC)

The American Academy of Pediatrics includes pretend characters and objects among materials inviting imagination and interaction. ASHA recognises communication beyond speech. Bringing these ideas together inspires this activity; it does not establish that a story box treats language delay. (American Academy of Pediatrics) (ASHA)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Choose two or three large, robust, safe objects: a figure, a vehicle and a box without a heavy lid. Avoid plastic bags, cords and miniatures.
  2. Let the child explore and choose. An object may become a character, scenery or simply something to examine; do not immediately correct its use.
  3. Start with one action: “the figure is going out”. Pause for a gesture, sound, choice, word or message from the child.
  4. Introduce a small event: “it started raining”. Offer a cloth as shelter and accept other solutions, including ending the story.
  5. Revisit the sequence if the child wishes: went out, met rain, found shelter. Photographs of objects may help without requiring a complete retelling.

What might this look like in a real situation?

The child puts a figure in the box and points at the car. The adult comments, “do you want a car ride?” and allows time to confirm or correct. The child moves the car: this already contributes to the story. A full sentence is not required for participation to count.

How can access be adapted?

At 2 or 3 years, one repeated action may be enough; this is not an age milestone or requirement. For older children, use age-respectful interests. With motor difficulties, a partner can move objects according to clear choices. Keep the child’s AAC system available from the start.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Notice decisions: choosing a character, starting an action, changing the plot or indicating “finished”. Check that adults leave genuine space rather than asking question after question. Story complexity is not an intelligence score, and this activity cannot establish a diagnosis.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Objects and responsive partners offer opportunities for shared choices and sequences.
  • A contribution can be an action, gesture, picture, sound or speech.

What we cannot say

  • Every child must invent a complete story for play to be worthwhile.
  • Pretend play alone proves or excludes autism or guarantees language progress.

Frequently asked questions

How can a child who does not speak join storytelling?

Offer objects and a way to choose. Describe actions, wait and respond to gestures, sounds or AAC without requiring speech.

How many objects should I use with a 2-year-old?

Start with a few safe objects and adjust to interest and ease of exploration. There is no compulsory number.

What if the child only lines objects up?

Join that exploration and offer an idea without imposing it. Lining up objects alone cannot support a diagnosis.

Must I correct the story order?

During play, accept unexpected plots. When teaching sequence, demonstrate a simple example and distinguish that goal from freedom to invent.
