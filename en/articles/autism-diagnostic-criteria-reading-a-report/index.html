Autism diagnostic criteria: how to read the report

An autism report should connect its conclusion to recognised criteria, but criteria are not a family checklist for diagnosis. Professionals apply them by integrating history, observation and context.

By the Talk+ team 18 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Which areas does DSM-5 describe?

DSM-5 criteria for autism include persistent differences across all three areas of social communication and interaction, plus at least two of four categories of restricted or repetitive patterns. They also consider onset during development and clinically significant impact. Examples illustrate criteria; they are not isolated points to count.

How should wording and specifiers be read?

A report may describe support needs, functional language, intellectual abilities or co-occurring conditions. These specifiers help describe a profile; they are not a rating of worth or an exact prediction of the future. ICD-11 uses a different classification system, so terminology may vary.

What can you ask the professional?

Ask which observations support each area of the criteria, what information came from home or school and what the child needs now. A question about one phrase does not automatically invalidate the whole report. If the explanation is unclear, request a feedback meeting or a plain-language summary.

In short

  • DSM-5 describes criteria across two core domains, considered together and in developmental context.
  • Criteria and specifiers organise clinical decisions; they are not a self-test or prognosis.

Conclusion

It is reasonable to want to understand how a diagnosis was reached. Ask how criteria, observed evidence, the child’s profile and support needs connect instead of deciding from isolated examples.
