Small-step teaching: when to fade prompts

Small-step teaching can make a task understandable without doing everything for the child. The challenge is choosing helpful support and noticing when a prompt is no longer needed.

Small-step teaching can make a task understandable without doing everything for the child. The challenge is choosing helpful support and noticing when a prompt is no longer needed.

Small-step teaching: what is known?

Education Endowment Foundation guidance brings together explicit instruction, guided practice and temporary scaffolding. Adults demonstrate, make supported attempts possible and create space for independent work. This is not a recipe reserved for one diagnosis. (Education Endowment Foundation)

NAEYC recommends achievable challenges and support matched to emerging skills. A communication system or motor adaptation may still be essential: fading a teaching prompt is different from removing access. (NAEYC) (ASHA)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Choose a meaningful action, such as putting a paintbrush away. Avoid broad goals such as “be more independent”.
  2. Split the task into visible actions: put the paper down, place the brush in its container and close the box. Teach the part that is difficult first.
  3. Demonstrate once using a few words. Wait for an attempt; repeating the instruction without a pause can hide what the child understood.
  4. If needed, point towards the container or offer a picture. Provide enough help without forcing the child’s hand or requiring a spoken repetition.
  5. On another occasion, try a less direct prompt. If difficulty returns, restore support and review the task rather than treating the mistake as refusal.

What might this look like in a real situation?

When tidying blocks, the child first puts a block in the box while the adult points. Later, the adult simply shows the open box. The useful change is understanding where pieces belong with less prompting, rather than finishing faster.

How can access be adapted?

A child with DLD may benefit from shorter sentences; another may need a picture, less noise or more time to organise movement. Use the observed profile, rather than a supposed rule for every autistic child or child with ADHD. Do not remove AAC, glasses, postural support or other access resources to “test independence”.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Record the action, prompt and context: “put two pieces away after I pointed”. Observe again on another occasion and with different materials. Less help on one day is useful teaching information, not a measure of overall development.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Clear models and suitable prompts can make a particular task more accessible.
  • Independence includes choosing support and asking for help.

What we cannot say

  • Every child should follow the same sequence or lose support at the same pace.
  • Removing communication or accessibility proves learning.

Frequently asked questions

How can I teach a task to a child with learning difficulties?

Define an observable action, demonstrate it and look for the least help that is sufficient. Review the goal with the team if difficulties persist.

When should I fade a prompt?

When the child shows they can attempt the task with less prompting. Fading should be gradual and reversible; access support need not disappear.

What if the child only starts when an adult points?

Check whether the instruction, materials and task starting point are clear. Try a less direct cue and allow time without leaving the child with an incomprehensible task.

Do app games automatically teach independence?

No. They may provide activities to explore, but a score does not demonstrate independence at home or school.
