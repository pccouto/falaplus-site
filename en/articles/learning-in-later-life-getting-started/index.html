Learning in later life: where to start

Learning in later life can begin with curiosity, a project or an everyday task. The learner should be involved in choosing.

Learning in later life can begin with curiosity, a project or an everyday task. The learner should be involved in choosing.

What supports this approach?

The NIH explains that ageing may make some learning slower without removing the ability to learn. Experiences vary, and age alone cannot define an individual’s potential. (NIH News in Health)

The W3C describes accessibility needs of older users. Adjusting presentation and operation can remove access barriers without making the content childish. (W3C Web Accessibility Initiative)

How can it be tried in practice?

The following steps are examples to adapt to the learner and context; they have not been evaluated as an intervention programme.

  1. Choose something meaningful: photography, music, reading or another area the learner wants to explore.
  2. Check access to seeing, hearing and handling materials. Adjust lighting, size, sound and response method.
  3. Introduce one part at a time and allow unhurried attempts without comparing people.
  4. Return with a small new challenge and ask what the learner would like to explore next.

How can progress be reviewed?

Observe interest, understanding and use of learning. Seek health advice for concerning recent changes or difficulties affecting daily life rather than treating them as inevitable ageing.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Older people can continue learning with suitable conditions.

What we cannot say

  • Everyone learns at the same pace or age explains every new difficulty.

Frequently asked questions

Can people learn after 65?

Yes. Goals and support should reflect preferences, experience and individual circumstances.

Should children’s materials be used to make it easier?

This is unnecessary. Adjust difficulty and access while keeping adult-appropriate topics and language.

How can I tell whether the approach helps?

Define an observable task, record the help needed and revisit understanding or application later. Speed alone does not measure learning.

Do these suggestions replace individual assessment?

No. Review adjustments with the learner and relevant professionals when difficulties persist or concerning changes appear.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
