Syllable games: blending and breaking words apart

Syllable games for children can start with spoken words before adding print. Hearing the parts of “sunset” and reading its written parts are related but different tasks.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • Blending and segmenting syllables explores the sound structure of words.
  • Syllable drills alone do not cover all reading skills or guarantee comprehension.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Syllables are sound units larger than phonemes. Combining “sun” and “set” blends syllables; breaking the spoken word apart segments them. Reading Rockets describes blending and segmenting activities. Syllable work does not replace teaching individual phonemes and their links to letters.

These examples are play suggestions, not a compulsory acquisition sequence. When adding print, use spelling patterns already taught at school. English syllables do not map onto a simple reusable set of written syllable cards in the same way as some other languages. Keep spoken sound and written spelling distinct.

References for this background: Reading Rockets — Blending and Segmenting Games · IES / What Works Clearinghouse — Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • Put the word togetherSay “sun… set” and ask the child to choose between a sunset picture and a shoe. Say the whole word naturally afterwards. Show the intended picture first if support is needed.
  • Word carriagesFor familiar spelling, write SUN and SET on two cards. Join them, read “sunset” and draw the scene. Separate the cards again to explore its parts.
  • One part changesCompare “sunset” and “sunshine” using pictures first, then print if accessible. Discuss which spoken part stays and which changes. Do not use pictures to guess unfamiliar written words.

Making the activity accessible

A child can point, move cards or use AAC rather than repeat aloud. Model an answer and reduce choices if needed. If clapping adds a motor barrier, point to large blocks instead. Use the pronunciation of the activity’s language, including natural regional variation.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Discuss persistent sound-awareness difficulties that affect learning with the teacher or speech and language therapist. A single error during play cannot identify a disorder.

Frequently asked questions

Are syllables letters?

No. A syllable is a spoken unit and can be represented by several letters.

Must the child already read?

Not for spoken games. Written cards need taught spelling patterns and appropriate help.

Can we use the child’s name?

Yes, if familiar; a long name may need more support to break apart.

Do these games treat dyslexia?

No. They are educational suggestions, not a replacement for assessment or individualised teaching.
