Does AAC stop a child from speaking?

What is known about augmentative and alternative communication, speech and participation.

What is known about augmentative and alternative communication, speech and participation.

The myth

“If a child uses pictures or a device, they will stop trying to speak.”

What the evidence says

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) offers ways to communicate that supplement or, when needed, replace speech. The systematic review by Pope et al. (2025) found promising outcomes in interventions that include AAC, but studies are varied and cannot guarantee the same result for every child. AAC should not be delayed while waiting for spoken language.

What we can say

  • Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) offers ways to communicate that supplement or, when needed, replace speech. The systematic review by Pope et al. (2025) found promising outcomes in interventions that include AAC, but studies are varied and cannot guarantee the same result for every child. AAC should not be delayed while waiting for spoken language.

What we cannot say

  • “If a child uses pictures or a device, they will stop trying to speak.”

What to do day to day

Recognise words, gestures, gaze, pictures and devices as ways to express messages. A team experienced in AAC can help choose an accessible system with useful vocabulary for daily routines and show communication partners how to model it without demanding a response.

Myth or fact: how to read the evidence

A study can show that two things occur together without showing that one caused the other. Look for research reviews, professional guidance and individual assessment; consider the population studied, limitations and source date.

Frequently asked questions

Does AAC stop a child from speaking?

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) offers ways to communicate that supplement or, when needed, replace speech. The systematic review by Pope et al. (2025) found promising outcomes in interventions that include AAC, but studies are varied and cannot guarantee the same result for every child. AAC should not be delayed while waiting for spoken language.

Does an association mean that one thing caused the other?

Not necessarily. Observational studies can identify relationships but do not establish cause on their own. Study design, other factors and the full body of evidence matter.

When should I seek advice?

If a concern persists, affects participation or skills are lost, speak with the child’s doctor or paediatrician and ask what assessment may be appropriate.

What can I do next?
