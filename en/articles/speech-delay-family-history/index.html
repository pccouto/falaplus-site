Speech delay in the family: is it hereditary?

“Dad didn’t talk until he was 3 and he’s fine.” This is often said to reassure. But family history tells us the opposite of what it seems: it is one of the factors that most raises the chances of speech delay, and so it is a reason to stay alert.

Is speech delay hereditary? In this article we explain what is known about family history and genetics in speech and language difficulties, and how to use that information to act earlier. See also Causes of speech delay.

In short

  • Having close relatives with speech delay, DLD or dyslexia raises the chances of delay.
  • Twin studies show an important genetic component in language difficulties.
  • Genetics raises the chances but does not decide the child’s path on its own.
  • “Dad talked late too” is a reason to watch earlier, not to wait.

What do studies show?

In a large population study, a family history of speech or language difficulties was among the factors most strongly linked to late talking at 24 months (Zubrick and colleagues, 2007). Twin studies show that when one identical twin has a language disorder, the other has it more often than in non-identical twins, pointing to an important genetic component (Bishop, North and Donlan, 1995).

There is no single “speech gene”. Many genetic factors, each with a small effect, interact with the environment.

What counts as family history?

  • Parents, siblings, aunts or uncles who talked late or had speech therapy.
  • Relatives with developmental language disorder (see Speech delay and DLD).
  • Relatives with dyslexia or reading difficulties (see Speech delay and dyslexia).
  • Stuttering in the family (see Childhood stuttering).
  • Relatives with autism or other neurodevelopmental conditions.

What can be said

  • Family history raises the chances of speech delay.
  • It is useful to share it with the doctor and therapist.
  • It justifies watching language more closely.

What cannot be said

  • “If Dad talked late and was fine, the child will be too.”
  • “If it is genetic, nothing can be done.”
  • “No family history means no risk.”

What to do with this information

  1. Tell the doctor and speech and language therapist who in the family had difficulties and what kind.
  2. Watch for warning signs from early on (see Warning signs by age).
  3. Do not wait to ask for an assessment if the child says little at 18–24 months.
  4. Use interaction strategies at home now (see 8 strategies for home).
  5. Later on, keep an eye on learning to read (see Phonological awareness).

Frequently asked questions about speech delay and family history

Is speech delay hereditary?

There is an important genetic component: having relatives with speech or language difficulties raises the chances, but does not make it certain.

Dad talked late too. Should I wait?

No. Family history is a reason to watch more closely and ask for an assessment early.

If one sibling had speech delay, will the other?

Not necessarily, but the chances are higher. It is worth watching for warning signs.

If it is genetic, does therapy help?

Yes. Genetics does not stop support and speech and language therapy from making a difference.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
