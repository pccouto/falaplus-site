Speech therapy at home: supporting communication

When a child starts speech therapy, it is natural for parents to wonder what they can do at home to help.

By the Talk+ team 17 October 2026 5 min read
Loading reads…

When a child starts speech therapy, it is natural for parents to wonder what they can do at home to help.

The answer is not to transform every moment of the day into a therapeutic session. Family involvement can consist of small strategies integrated into games, conversations and usual routines.

The most important thing is that there is collaboration between the child, the family and the therapist.

What is the role of the speech and language therapist?

A speech and language therapist assesses and supports difficulties related to communication, language, speech, voice, fluency and other areas within their professional scope.

In childhood, you can work on very different goals, such as:

  • Develop understanding of language.
  • Increase the possibilities of functional communication.
  • Support the production of sounds and words.
  • Develop sentence construction.
  • Work on conversation skills.
  • Introduce or adapt augmentative and alternative communication systems.

The strategies depend on the difficulties identified, the age, interests and abilities of the child.

Why is family participation important?

Therapy sessions represent only a part of the child's communicative experiences.

At home, there are countless opportunities to use skills that are being developed with professional support.

The family can help transfer these learnings to real situations, as long as they understand the objectives and know how to apply the strategies.

For example, if the child is working on functional requests, snack time may allow them to use “more”, “water”, “help” or “it’s over”.

These opportunities must take place without pressure and respecting the child’s needs.

Six strategies to use at home

1. Follow the child's interests

A child interested in animals may be more willing to participate in a game with animal figures than in an activity that does not arouse their curiosity.

Motivation can facilitate participation.

2. Model language

Modeling means presenting examples of communication that the child can observe and eventually use.

If you are pushing a car, the adult can say: “The car moves” or “Go quickly”.

It is not necessary to immediately ask for a repeat.

3. Expand the words

If the child says “dog”, they can respond “The dog is sleeping”.

Thus, it offers a slightly more elaborate model, maintaining the meaning of the original message.

4. Take breaks

In a familiar song, you can pause briefly before a familiar word and see if the child wants to participate.

You can do this through a word, gesture, movement or other means of communication.

If he doesn't respond, continue the activity without turning the break into a demand.

5. Use different forms of communication

Gestures, signs, photographs and AAC systems can help the child participate.

The objective is to make communication more accessible, not to force the child to use speech exclusively.

6. Recognize small achievements

A choice made autonomously, a new attempt to initiate interaction or using a card to ask for help can represent significant progress.

Value participation and not just correct pronunciation.

What to avoid?

It is important to avoid some practices that can increase frustration:

  • Correct all pronounced words.
  • Asking successive questions without giving time to respond.
  • Force the child to repeat when they show discomfort.
  • Compare your progress with that of other children.
  • Use articulation exercises not recommended by the therapist.

In the case of certain motor speech disorders, such as apraxia, specific practice must be carefully guided.

How do you know if there is progress?

Progress does not always translate immediately into an increase in the number of words.

The child may begin to understand certain instructions better, make more choices, ask for help or initiate interactions more frequently.

The family can share these observations with the therapist, including difficulties that continue to arise outside of the session.

It is useful to define concrete and functional objectives, adapting them regularly to the child's evolution.

Conclusion

Communication support does not end when a speech therapy session ends.

Family participation can help bring learning to the environments where the child lives, plays and communicates.

The goal is not to require more exercise, but to create meaningful opportunities for interaction.

The family does not need to replace the therapist. Your role is to help the child communicate in everyday experiences.

Informative content. It does not replace an individual assessment or guidance from a healthcare professional.

Frequently asked questions

How to support speech therapy at home?

Follow the therapist's instructions, use functional objectives in routines and games and respect the child's interests and rhythm.

Is it necessary to transform routines into exercises?

No. Small, natural opportunities for choice, conversation, and participation can be more meaningful than requiring constant training.

Should I correct all the words?

No. Continuous correction can increase pressure. The therapist can indicate how to model the appropriate form for that child's goals.

How do you know if there is progress?

Also note understanding, choices, requests for help, initiatives and participation, and share these observations with the therapist.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
