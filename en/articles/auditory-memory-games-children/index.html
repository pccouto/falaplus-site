Auditory memory games: words and instructions

Auditory memory games involve recalling information heard. When a child does not follow an instruction, consider whether they heard it, understood its words and could perform the action.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • Specific messages can be practised with appropriate support for recall.
  • Missing instructions does not prove a memory deficit, and list practice does not guarantee gains across all tasks.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Humber Teaching NHS provides auditory processing and memory activities. Such guidance helps organise listening experiences; a home activity cannot separate every skill involved. Remembering familiar words differs from understanding a long new sentence.

Visual support is not cheating. It can make a task accessible and reduce the information held in memory. Removing pictures to prove that a child can listen is not necessary. Hearing concerns need assessment rather than louder sound or an expanding exercise list.

References for this background: Humber Teaching NHS Foundation Trust — Auditory Processing and Memory Games · Children and Family Health Devon — Supporting listening skills: activities to try.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • A small orderWith familiar objects visible, request “the ball”, then “the ball and the car” if accessible. The child can hand them over or point. Start successfully and do not lengthen every round.
  • A message with a destinationChoose a short message for a toy: “The soup is ready”. Say it, allow another listen and deliver it through speech, cards or an agreed gesture.
  • Listen and organisePresent two familiar action pictures and say the order: “wash, dry”. The child orders or indicates them. Act it out afterwards; visible pictures mean the activity includes visual support.

Making the activity accessible

Reduce background noise, check vocabulary and give one instruction at a time. Allow repetition and prompts without penalties. Accept alternative responses when speech or movement is difficult. Participation and understanding matter more than enduring ever longer lists.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Seek advice when difficulty with familiar instructions persists and affects daily life. Note effects of noise, length and vocabulary and discuss these with school and a speech and language therapist. Suspected hearing problems need hearing assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Is auditory memory the same as hearing?

No. Hearing, understanding and recalling are related but distinct processes.

Can I repeat an instruction?

Yes. Repetition and prompts can make play accessible without penalties.

Must I hide the pictures?

No. Visible pictures may support participation. Removing them changes the demand.

Can these games diagnose auditory processing disorder?

No. Auditory processing concerns need appropriate professional assessment, not a home game.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
