Visual routines: helping a child understand what will happen

A sequence of pictures can make it visible what happens first and next. Visual routines for children show activities, steps or changes through objects, photos, drawings, words or other symbols. They may be useful for some children, but are not needed or suitable in the same way for everyone.

> In brief: start with one real routine, use symbols the child understands and keep the sequence flexible. A visual support provides information and supports participation; it should not force a child to complete a task without choice.

What is a visual routine?

It might be a short sequence of photos showing how to leave home, a “first/then” card, a written list or objects that show what is coming. There is no single format. A child may understand real objects, photographs, symbols, words or a combination. If it is unclear whether an image is understood, show the object or explain it.

A visual routine is not necessarily a rigid timetable for the entire day. For one family, a two-step sequence is enough. Another may find it useful to show one transition, such as “play — tidy up — lunch”. The format should solve a real need without creating unnecessary work.

How can you introduce a visual sequence?

  1. Choose a specific situation. For example, packing a bag or washing hands. Avoid planning the whole day at once.
  2. Notice how the child understands best. Objects, photos, pictograms, words or gestures may be options. If a picture is not recognised, show the object or explain it.
  3. Show the sequence before and during the activity. Point to the current and next step, using simple words if helpful.
  4. Include ways to communicate. Depending on the situation, the child may point to “break”, “help”, “finished” or “no”.
  5. Update the plan when it changes. If an activity is cancelled, explain and change the support. Do not leave a sequence that you know is wrong.
  6. Notice whether it helps. Ask through the child’s preferred communication and observe whether the support makes participation easier. If not, simplify, change or stop using it.

How can adults show changes?

A visual support can show that something will be different, but it cannot guarantee that a child will accept the change or feel calm. Explain what changed and what remains the same. For example: “We are not going to the park today; we are going to the library. Then we go home.” Give notice when possible without promising something that may change again.

If a change is unexpected, show the new sequence when possible. The child may need time, sensory support, a break or another communication method. Do not use pictures to disguise a change, persuade a child or remove the option to refuse when there is a choice.

Examples for home and school

  • Morning: wake up → get dressed → breakfast → leave. The child might choose between two suitable tops.
  • Washing hands: wet hands → soap → rinse → dry. If a step is uncomfortable, discuss an adaptation.
  • Classroom: individual activity → break → group task. Show changes to the plan when possible.
  • Appointment: arrive → wait → talk/play → leave. A “break” or “finished” card may be helpful.

These are examples, not prescriptions. The number of steps, language and symbols depend on the child and the actual routine.

What should adults avoid?

  • Making the sequence so detailed it is hard to use.
  • Using it as a threat or reward for obedience.
  • Insisting that every step be completed in exactly the same way.
  • Leaving it outdated when plans change.
  • Treating a need for visual support as proof of a diagnosis.

Predictable routines may support understanding and organisation, but needs and responses differ. Autistic children are not defined by whether they like routines; individual preferences matter.

Conclusion

A visual routine is a communication support that can make a sequence more concrete. Starting with one simple situation, choosing understandable symbols and allowing flexibility can help keep it useful. The child’s response should guide whether to continue, adapt or stop using it.

Frequently asked questions

Does every autistic child need a visual routine?

No. Needs and preferences vary. Try supports flexibly and keep them only if they are useful to the child.

Which pictures should I use?

Choose representations the child recognises. Photos, objects, symbols, words or gestures may work; there is no universal set.

Does a visual schedule stop a child making choices?

It should not. It can show what is planned and include choices, a break, help or refusal where appropriate.

Will a visual routine prevent meltdowns?

No outcome can be guaranteed. It may help some children understand a sequence, but discomfort, pain, overload or other factors need separate attention.

How should I change a routine chart when plans change?

Explain what changed, update the pictures and show what happens now. Allow time or offer a break or another way to communicate.
