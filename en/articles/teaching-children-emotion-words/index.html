Teaching emotions: words for what a child feels

“You’re angry because the tower fell down.” A sentence like this, said calmly, does more than it seems: it gives the child a word for something they feel in their body and shows them someone understands. It is the beginning of emotion vocabulary.

Being able to name feelings helps children say what is going on, ask for help and, little by little, calm down. In this article we explain what research says about teaching emotions to children and suggest simple everyday ideas, with and without words.

In short

  • Talking about emotions with children is linked to better emotional understanding and more helping behaviour.
  • First the adult names the feeling; the child gradually learns to recognise and say it.
  • Pictures, gestures and cards let children express emotions before they can say them.
  • Accepting the feeling does not mean accepting every behaviour.

Why does naming emotions help?

In a study with toddlers, parents who talked more about emotions, asking and explaining how characters in a book felt, had children who helped and shared more (Brownell and colleagues, 2013). Another line of research showed that families who accept and name emotions, known as “emotion coaching”, have children with better regulation (Gottman, Katz and Hooven, 1996).

For many children with communication difficulties, an emotion without words can come out as crying, a tantrum or withdrawal (see Challenging behaviour and communication breakdown).

Everyday ideas

  • Narrate the moment“You’re sad because Grandad left.” Short, calm, without too many questions.
  • Books and drawingsStop at a picture and ask: “How does he feel? Why?”
  • Mirror facesMake happy, angry, scared and surprised faces together.
  • Feelings thermometerA scale with colours or pictures to show “a little” or “a lot”.
  • Feelings cardsPictures the child can point to or hand over when they cannot talk.
  • Start with a few emotions: happy, sad, angry, scared. Add others gradually (tired, frustrated, proud).
  • Name your own feelings too: “I’m a bit nervous about the traffic.”
  • Talk about feelings in calm moments, not only in difficult ones.
  • Accept the feeling and set limits on behaviour: “You can be angry. You can’t hit.”

What if the child does not talk yet?

Visual supports let children express emotions before speech. Keeping feelings cards to hand, at home and at school, and modelling them (“I’m happy”, pointing to the card) helps the child use them independently (see AAC modelling).

Frequently asked questions about teaching emotions

At what age can you teach emotions?

From very early on. With babies, the adult names feelings; around 2–3, many children start using words like “happy” and “sad”.

Which emotions should you teach first?

Happy, sad, angry and scared. Then others such as tired, frustrated or proud.

My child does not talk. How can they say how they feel?

With picture cards, gestures or a communication app, modelled by adults in daily life.

Doesn’t naming the feeling reward the tantrum?

No. Acknowledging the emotion helps the child calm down; limits on behaviour stay in place.
