The child does not want to take part in therapy: what can help?

When a child does not want to take part in therapy, the priority is to understand refusal, not defeat it. Pain, fear, separation, sensory overload, uncertainty or unsuitable demands may contribute.

What should be observed first?

Note when distress starts, how the child communicates discomfort, what happens beforehand and what helps recovery. Address possible health and safety needs when signs exist. “No” may be expressed through words, gestures, moving away or behaviour; all deserve a response.

How can participation be adapted?

The clinician may preview the sequence, offer genuine choices, reduce demand, adjust the environment, allow breaks and build rapport through interests. Gradual entry or a trusted person’s presence may help. Adaptation does not abandon goals; it creates safer access.

When should you review or stop?

Intense or persistent distress, restraint, growing fear and lack of adaptation require immediate discussion. Ask what may explain refusal, which changes will be tried and when to review. If rapport does not recover or the approach is unsuitable, a pause or another option may be needed.

Frequently asked questions

Is crying normal in early sessions?

Some uncertainty may occur, but distress should not be ignored or treated as a requirement.

Should a child be forced to enter?

Force can increase fear and does not explain the cause. Safety and adaptation should guide the response.

Will a reward solve refusal?

It may briefly change behaviour without addressing pain, fear, overload or uncertainty.

Can I ask for an adjustment session?

Yes. Discuss gradual entry and clear criteria for judging whether it helps.
