A child may know an answer but be unable to say it quickly, understand instructions better when they are shown, or need augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) to take part. At school, speech and language therapy is most useful when it addresses these real barriers and connects with classroom activities.

What can a speech and language therapist work on?

Depending on assessment and professional scope, support may address language comprehension and expression, speech, fluency, voice, social communication, early literacy and AAC. The aim is not to make a child “more compliant” or force speech; it is to improve functional communication, access to learning and participation.

A single difficulty does not determine a plan. The team looks at different situations — explaining an idea, following instructions, chatting at break, asking for help — and identifies strengths, barriers and priorities with the child and family.

From a session to classroom life

Support may be individual, in a small group, in class, or through consultation and planning with teachers. No one format is always best. The choice depends on the goal, context and where the skill needs to be used; some learning calls for focused practice, while other goals benefit from natural opportunities throughout the day.

A useful goal describes observable participation: for example, choosing materials, responding in a science activity with speech, gesture or AAC, or asking for clarification. Adults agree how to model, allow response time and acknowledge different ways of communicating.

Collaborating without overloading the child

Family, teacher and therapist can share brief examples and agree on a small set of consistent strategies. A child should not have to give up their communication system to “prove” they can speak. Nor should adults assume that a strategy works just because it was practised in a quiet room.

In Portugal, Resource Centres for Inclusion may provide specialist support in pedagogical partnership with schools, according to assessment and the measures put in place. Availability and organisation vary. The school can explain who supports the child, which goals were agreed and how progress will be reviewed.

  • Describe the barrier and situation instead of labelling a child uninterested.
  • Keep AAC available during lessons, breaks, meals and trips when it is part of the child’s communication.
  • Review goals with the child and family, taking preferences and wellbeing into account.
  • Check whether a skill appears in real settings and adjust support if it does not generalise.

In short

  • School speech and language therapy should support communication, curriculum access and participation in school life.
  • Individual sessions, classroom work and consultation can complement each other; the model depends on the child and the goal.

In summary

Effective collaboration turns therapy goals into everyday opportunities to communicate and learn. Speech is one way to communicate, not the only measure of participation.

Frequently asked questions

Is this support the same for every pupil?

No. Support should reflect each pupil’s goals, barriers and preferences, and be reviewed in light of what happens in school.

Who can answer questions?

Start with the class teacher and ask who coordinates the support and when it will be reviewed. The multidisciplinary team may take part in the processes set out in Portuguese law.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
