The importance of play in child development

Play is a right, a pleasure and a way of discovering the world. Find out what research says, what kinds of play exist and how adults can join in without taking control of the game.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 6 min read
Loading reads…

People often talk about the importance of play, and rightly so. But what does research actually say about play and child development? In this guide we explain why play matters, what kinds of play exist, what science knows (and does not yet know) and how adults can support it.

In short

  • Play is a child’s right and has value in itself, not only for what it teaches.
  • There are many ways to play, and all of them can be meaningful to a child.
  • Research shows links between play, language and learning, but promises no magic effects.
  • Adults help most when they join in rather than direct.

Play is a right, not just a means to an end

Before any benefit, there is a principle: the right to play is recognised in Article 31 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child. Play is valuable for the enjoyment, the choice and the discovery it brings. It does not need to be «for» something to be important.

This matters because when every game becomes an exercise, we risk losing what makes play valuable: the child’s initiative and enjoyment.

Types of play: what are they?

«Play» is not just one thing. These are some of the most commonly described types of play:

  • Sensory explorationTouching, shaking, filling and emptying, finding out what happens when…
  • ConstructionStacking, building, drawing and doing puzzles.
  • Physical playRunning, jumping, climbing, dancing and balancing.
  • Social playGames with rules, taking turns, cooperating and negotiating.
  • Pretend playA box that becomes a car, playing house or playing doctors.
  • Guided playThe child chooses; the adult sets up the space and makes suggestions.

There is also free play outdoors, playing alone or alongside other children, and digital play, each with its own features. These are not compulsory stages: every child combines them in their own way, and repetitive, sensory or solitary play is meaningful too. Read more about pretend play.

Benefits of play: what does science say?

Play offers opportunities to practise and combine many skills: communicating, thinking, moving and relating to others. Three research syntheses help show how far the evidence goes:

Pretend play and language. A meta-analysis of 35 studies, with around 6,800 children, found a small-to-moderate association between symbolic play and language.

Quinn, Donnelly and Kidd, 2018

Guided play and learning. Compared with direct instruction, guided play had advantages for specific outcomes (early maths, shape knowledge and flexibility in switching tasks), but not for every outcome studied.

Skene and colleagues, 2022

Pretend play and self-regulation. A 2026 meta-analysis, with around 2,900 children, found a small association between pretend play and executive functions, such as controlling impulses and keeping information in mind.

Constien and colleagues, 2026

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes play, especially with attentive adults, as a central part of healthy development, and recommends that paediatricians promote it with families.

What science does not (yet) say

An association shows that two things tend to go together; it does not prove that one causes the other. Knowing the limits of the evidence protects us from exaggerated promises, including those made by toys and apps.

What we can say

  • Play gives context for communicating and practising skills.
  • Symbolic play and language are related.
  • Guided play helped with specific outcomes.

What we cannot say

  • That play «develops every area of the brain».
  • That pretend play, on its own, makes a child more intelligent.
  • That play replaces assessment or therapy.

What is the adult’s role in play?

  1. Offer time, space and simple materials. Boxes, cloths, blocks and everyday objects have many uses.
  2. Watch before joining in. What interests the child? How are they playing?
  3. Join in without taking over. Follow their rules, imitate and comment.
  4. Respond to communication, whether a gesture, a sound, a word or a picture, without turning the game into a test.
  5. Value enjoyment and connection, not the number of words or the «level» of play.

You will find practical ideas in Activities to encourage speech.

Free play and tablet games: are they the same?

No. A digital game has rules and goals set by the game itself; free play with objects and people is open-ended and led by the child. Digital games can be part of the day, but they do not replace free play. What makes the difference is the content, the time, whether an adult is there and what the screen replaces, such as sleep, movement or conversation. See also Benefits of educational games.

Frequently asked questions about the importance of play

Why is play important in child development?

Play is enjoyable and offers chances to communicate, think, move and build relationships. In early childhood, play with attentive adults is considered a central part of healthy development. Research finds links with language and self-regulation, but does not promise guaranteed effects.

How much time a day should a child play?

There is no single number that suits every child. What matters is free time every day for play the child chooses, including outdoor play, movement and play with other people.

Is it bad if a child always plays the same game?

No. Repetition is a common way of playing and of mastering an activity. A type of play, on its own, neither confirms nor rules out any difficulty. If you have other concerns about development, talk to a professional.

Are educational toys better?

Not necessarily. Simple, open-ended toys such as blocks, boxes or cloths allow many different games. Often, the most valuable thing is an adult who is available to play.

Does playing on a tablet count as play?

It can be part of the day, but it is not the same as free play with objects and people. What makes the difference is the content, the time, whether an adult is there and what the screen replaces, such as sleep, movement or conversation.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
