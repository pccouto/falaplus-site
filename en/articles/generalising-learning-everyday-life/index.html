Generalising learning to everyday life

Generalising learning means using something learned with different people, materials or situations. Selecting a picture on a tablet does not guarantee recognising its real-world object.

Generalising learning means using something learned with different people, materials or situations. Selecting a picture on a tablet does not guarantee recognising its real-world object.

Generalising learning: what is known?

ASHA describes early intervention around participation in children’s routines and environments. This supports looking for meaningful uses of learning rather than accumulating answers in one exercise. The guidance does not establish the effectiveness of a particular app. (ASHA)

Varied examples and observations across situations help adults understand what is accessible to a child. EEF discusses examples and guided practice; NAEYC recommends gathering information in different contexts. Neither principle means that practice automatically transfers. (Education Endowment Foundation) (NAEYC)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Choose a useful goal: recognising “cup” to join snack time rather than memorising a list without a purpose.
  2. Start with a familiar object. Name it while using it and accept looking, pointing, touching, speech or AAC as possible responses.
  3. Offer another cup with a different colour or size. Change just one feature if the new example makes the task difficult.
  4. Invite another person to join in or try another space. Keep the communication and motor adaptations the child needs.
  5. Revisit the opportunity on another day without demanding an immediate answer. If it does not happen, demonstrate again and investigate the barrier.

What might this look like in a real situation?

After exploring a picture of a spoon, the child helps choose a spoon to stir a drink. Later, they find a different spoon among safe utensils. Naming a picture, choosing an object and using it are different observations; describe each in terms of what actually happened.

How can access be adapted?

For a child who benefits from predictability, explain the change: “another cup, also for drinking”. Avoid changing the person, room, noise and materials together. For multilingual children, keep the languages used in everyday relationships. A word learned in one language need not appear immediately in another.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Note the word or action, materials, person, support and situation. Look for real examples without making every meal a test. If a child loses skills or difficulties continue to concern family or school, seek professional assessment rather than expecting more game practice to resolve them.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Varied examples and natural opportunities allow adults to teach and observe different uses.
  • It is useful to notice participation with different people and materials.

What we cannot say

  • A correct tablet response guarantees understanding, speech or independence elsewhere.
  • A different response proves poor effort or loss of learning.

Frequently asked questions

Why can a child answer in a game but not elsewhere?

Cues, pictures, language and context may differ. Examine those differences before concluding that the child does not know or will not cooperate.

How can tablet learning connect to real life?

Connect a picture to an object and an action that interest the child. Vary examples gradually and observe without promising transfer.

Should I repeat until every context is successful?

Do not turn routines into endless drills. Offer brief, meaningful opportunities, respect breaks and adjust support.

Does generalisation mean doing everything unaided?

No. A child can use learning with AAC, motor adaptations or chosen assistance. Functional participation is what matters.
