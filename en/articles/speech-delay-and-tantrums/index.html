Speech delay and tantrums: when frustration is a lack of words

The child wants the blue cup, not the red one. They point, shout, throw themselves on the floor. To the adult it is a tantrum; to the child it may be the only way to say “not that one”. When words are missing, frustration finds other outlets.

The link between speech delay and tantrums is a common worry for families. In this article we explain what studies show, how to tell communication frustration from other reasons and what helps reduce tantrums. See also Challenging behaviour and communication breakdown.

In short

  • Young children with fewer words tend to have more frequent and more intense tantrums.
  • Many tantrums are a way of communicating when words do not come.
  • Giving the child ways to ask, refuse and choose reduces frustration.
  • Very frequent or long tantrums, or self-injury, are worth discussing with a professional.

What do studies show?

In a study of more than 2,000 children aged 12 to 38 months, those with a smaller expressive vocabulary had more frequent and more intense tantrums than same-age children with more words (Manning and colleagues, 2019). The authors stress that tantrums are normal at this age; what differs is how often and how intense they are.

Why does a lack of words cause frustration?

  • Cannot askThey know what they want but do not have the word to say it.
  • Cannot refuseWithout “no”, “all done” or “stop”, refusal comes out as crying or pushing.
  • Not understoodThey try to say it, but adults do not understand, and repeating does not work.
  • Cannot explainPain, fear or tiredness are hard to show without words.

What helps reduce tantrums

  • Teach simple ways to ask and refuse: signs (“more”, “all done”), pictures or short words.
  • Offer choices before frustration builds: “Blue cup or red?”.
  • Name what the child feels: “You’re angry because you want the blue one” (see Teaching emotions).
  • Prepare for changes with visual routines (see Visual routines).
  • During a tantrum, say little and stay calm; talk afterwards, when the child is settled.
  • Value every attempt to communicate, even imperfect ones.

Talk to a professional if tantrums

  • are very frequent (several times a day) or very long;
  • include head banging, self-biting or hurting others;
  • also happen with people and in places where the child is usually calm;
  • come with other developmental warning signs.

Frequently asked questions about speech delay and tantrums

Does speech delay cause tantrums?

Not on its own, but children with fewer words tend to have more frequent and intense tantrums, out of frustration.

The child hits because they cannot talk. What should I do?

Keep everyone safe, stay calm and afterwards teach another way to say the same thing, such as a sign or a picture.

Do tantrums lessen when the child starts talking?

Often, yes. Having ways to communicate, with or without words, helps reduce frustration.

Can frequent tantrums be a sign of autism?

Not on their own. But if there are other signs in interaction or communication, an assessment is worthwhile.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
