Speech, language and communication: what is the difference?

We often say a child «doesn’t talk» when, in fact, they communicate a lot. Understanding the difference helps you observe more closely and explain your concerns to a professional.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 4 min read
Loading reads…

The difference between speech and language, and between both and communication, underpins any conversation with a speech and language therapist or paediatrician. In this article we explain the three concepts using professional terminology, give examples and show how to observe a child day to day.

In short

  • Communication is the bigger circle: it includes looks, gestures, sounds, pictures and words.
  • Language is the system of words and rules: spoken, written or signed.
  • Speech is producing sounds: articulation, voice, rhythm and fluency.
  • The right support depends on what is affected, and only an assessment can tell.

Communication: the bigger circle

To communicate is to share and understand intentions. A child communicates when they look at a toy and then at the adult, point, wave, smile, cry for attention, hand over an object to ask for help, or use signs, pictures or a device. Communication exists before words and goes beyond them.

Language: the system of words and rules

  • VocabularyWords and their meanings.
  • GrammarHow we combine and change words: «cat», «cats», «the cat ate».
  • NarrativeTelling what happened, in order.
  • PragmaticsUsing language with other people: asking, commenting, taking turns, adjusting to context.

Language can be spoken, written or signed (sign language is a full language) and has two sides: receptive language, understanding what is heard, read or seen, and expressive language, sharing ideas through words, gestures, signs or pictures.

Speech: producing sounds

Speech is the motor and sound part: articulating sounds, voice, rhythm and fluency. Examples of speech difficulties include swapping or dropping sounds («tat» for «cat»), stuttering, difficulty planning speech movements (childhood apraxia of speech) or unclear speech due to neuromotor difficulties (dysarthria).

A child can have good language and speech difficulties, or clear speech and language difficulties.

A child can have more than one of these difficulties. When language difficulties are persistent, affect daily life and no other condition explains them, professionals speak of developmental language disorder (DLD), a term defined by international consensus (CATALISE). DLD is not caused by the family, by not reading at home or by bilingualism.

How to observe a child before talking to a professional

  1. What do they understand? Do they follow simple instructions? Recognise names of objects and people?
  2. How do they communicate? Pointing, gestures, sounds, words or pictures?
  3. Which words and sentences do they use? Do they combine words? Tell what happened?
  4. How do they sound? Is it easy to understand them? Are there sounds they always swap?
  5. Do they react to sounds and to people calling them?

Writing down concrete examples helps the professional. If there are warning signs, see Speech delay: warning signs and when to seek help.

Frequently asked questions about speech and language

Can a child who does not talk have good language?

Yes. They may understand a lot and communicate through gestures, signs or pictures. Not speaking does not mean not understanding.

Is stuttering a language problem?

Stuttering is mainly a difficulty with speech fluency. A child who stutters can have well-developed language.

Is it normal to swap sounds at age 3?

Some swaps are common in the early years and disappear with time; others call for an assessment. A speech and language therapist can say whether the child is on the expected path.

What is DLD?

Developmental language disorder: persistent difficulties that affect daily life, with no other condition to explain them. Only an assessment can identify it.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
