Letter games for children: connecting print and sounds

Letter and sound games for children work best when print connects with spoken words. Singing the alphabet is enjoyable, but it does not by itself show that a child can read.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • Connecting print with speech sounds gives alphabet games a purpose.
  • Recognising every letter does not equal reading, and a game cannot diagnose a difficulty.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Letter names and the sounds represented in words are different information. The letter M is named “em”; in “mat” it represents /m/. The IES recommends connecting sound awareness with letters and practising reading and writing rather than only recognising shapes.

English letters can represent different sounds, and a sound can have different spellings. Start with correspondences already taught instead of inventing universal rules. This guide focuses on the print–sound connection; the syllable guide explores spoken parts and the age-six guide moves into words and messages.

References for this background: IES / REL Southeast — Supporting Your Child’s Reading at Home · IES / What Works Clearinghouse — Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • Find an MLook for a familiar letter on packaging and a caption. Name it and demonstrate its sound in an accessible word. There is no need to introduce every typeface together.
  • Two letters, two cluesUsing taught M and S, say “map” and “sun”. The child points to the letter representing the first sound. Model the process before offering another word.
  • A letter changes the messageRead “mat” and “sat” if these patterns are known. Swap the first letter and discuss the new meaning. Pictures can confirm meaning after reading, rather than replace decoding.

Making the activity accessible

Use large cards and few choices. A child can respond through pointing, gaze or an agreed access method. Producing a difficult speech sound is not required to demonstrate recognition. A beginner can participate by watching and listening to a model.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Share concrete examples with the teacher if print–sound links remain very difficult despite suitable teaching. Occasional early letter confusion does not establish dyslexia.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use letter names or sounds?

Both have a role. State which you mean; blending needs the sound correspondences being taught.

Uppercase or lowercase first?

Coordinate with school and introduce needed forms gradually, without too many choices.

Must the child copy many letters?

Not to join these games. Recognition, handwriting and reading are related but distinct tasks.

Does reversing B and D mean dyslexia?

An isolated reversal does not diagnose dyslexia. Persistent patterns, learning history and assessment matter.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
