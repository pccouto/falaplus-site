Reading comprehension: reads words but does not understand

Reading comprehension means building meaning from text. A child may say the words readily yet need help understanding a cause, connecting events or interpreting an expression.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • Comprehension strategies can be taught and supported explicitly.
  • Accurate reading aloud does not guarantee understanding, and one missed question cannot diagnose a condition.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

ASHA distinguishes word reading from language comprehension. Vocabulary, sentence structure, topic knowledge and connecting ideas can all matter. Reading quickly or pronouncing every word correctly does not describe the whole process.

Compare situations without assigning a label: does comprehension improve when an adult reads? Is a short sentence accessible while a paragraph is not? Are key words unfamiliar? Such observations guide support and discussion with school; they do not themselves identify hyperlexia, dyslexia or another condition.

References for this background: ASHA — Written Language Disorders · IES / What Works Clearinghouse — Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • One sentence, one actionRead “Rita put on her coat because it was cold”. Act out the scene and discuss why. Accept choosing a picture or using AAC instead of requiring a long spoken explanation.
  • Clue detectivesChoose a short text and a question answered explicitly in it. Find the helpful phrase together. Then compare with a question that requires connecting two clues.
  • Three momentsAfter a short passage, draw its beginning, what changed and the ending. Revisit the text to check. The goal is organising meaning, not recalling every word without looking.

Making the activity accessible

Explain an important word first, split the text and pause for discussion. Keep content interesting for the child’s age even when reading demands need simplifying. An adult can read some sections; access support does not remove learning.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Discuss persistent difficulties restricting learning or participation with the teacher and, where needed, a speech and language therapist. Bring sample texts, responses and supports. Do not focus only on reading speed or quantity without understanding the barrier.

What we can conclude

What we can say

  • Comprehension strategies can be taught and supported explicitly.

What we cannot say

  • Accurate reading aloud does not guarantee understanding, and one missed question cannot diagnose a condition.

Frequently asked questions

Does fast reading mean understanding?

Not necessarily. Recognising words and building meaning are related but different dimensions.

Should I ask for rereading?

It can help with a clear purpose and support. Repetition without addressing the difficulty can increase frustration.

Can I read the text aloud?

Yes. It enables content discussion and observation when decoding places fewer demands on the child.

Is this hyperlexia?

That profile cannot be inferred from this difficulty alone. Individual developmental and learning assessment is needed.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
