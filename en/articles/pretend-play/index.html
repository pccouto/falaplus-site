Pretend play: what research says about symbolic play

A box becomes a car, a spoon becomes a phone and teddy has a temperature and needs the doctor. Find out what research knows, and does not yet know, about this way of playing.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Pretend play, also called symbolic play or make-believe, is one of the most studied forms of play. We explain what it is, how it relates to language and how adults can support it without taking over the story.

In short

  • Pretend play means using one thing to stand for another, or acting «as if».
  • It is associated with language, but research does not prove that, on its own, it moves language forward.
  • It is a rich setting for conversation, especially with an adult who follows the child’s story.
  • A type of play, on its own, does not diagnose anything.

What is symbolic play? Examples

  • Objects that become othersA block that is a phone, a box that is a boat.
  • Pretend actionsDrinking from an empty cup, feeding soup to a doll.
  • RolesBeing the parent, the teacher, the doctor or the firefighter.
  • StoriesCreating scenes and plots, alone or with others.

Pretend play can be verbal, but also gestural, or supported by signs, pictures and AAC. You do not need to speak to pretend.

Pretend play and language: is there a link?

A meta-analysis of 35 studies, with around 6,800 children, found a small-to-moderate relationship between symbolic play and language.

Quinn, Donnelly and Kidd, 2018

During pretend play, adults and children took more conversational turns than in other kinds of play.

Creaghe and colleagues, 2021

But be careful: association does not prove cause. The two skills may develop together, or other factors may influence both. An influential critical review concluded that claims that pretend play, on its own, causes developmental gains go beyond the available evidence.

What about executive functions?

Executive functions include controlling impulses, keeping information in mind and changing strategy. A 2026 meta-analysis, with around 2,900 children, found a small association between pretend play and these functions. Here too, the data are correlational.

In short: pretend play is a rich setting for communicating and interacting. It is not guaranteed «training» for language or intelligence.

How to support pretend play

  • Step into the child’s story. If teddy is ill, ask «where does it hurt?» rather than changing the plot.
  • Give voices to characters and feelings: «Teddy is sad. Does he want a hug?»
  • Offer open-ended objects: boxes, cloths, lids, old clothes.
  • Name actions and sequences: «First we wash, then we dry.»
  • Let the child lead. The goal is play, not a lesson.

Myths about pretend play

What we know

  • Playing alone or repeating the same game is still play.
  • Every child plays in their own way and at their own pace.
  • Pretend play can happen with gestures, signs or pictures.

Myths

  • «If they don’t pretend, they must be autistic.»
  • «The more pretend play, the more intelligent.»
  • «A tablet game is the same thing.»

Frequently asked questions about pretend play

At what age does pretend play start?

The first simple symbolic actions, such as pretending to drink or feeding a doll, usually appear in the second year of life, with wide variation between children.

Is symbolic play the same as pretend play?

In everyday use, yes: both describe pretending that an object, an action or a role stands for something else. You may also see «imaginative play» or «make-believe».

Which toys support pretend play?

Kitchens, dolls, animals and vehicles and, above all, simple objects that can be «anything», such as boxes and cloths.

Are digital games pretend play?

Generally not. A game with set rules and goals is a different experience from free pretend play, where the child invents the story.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
