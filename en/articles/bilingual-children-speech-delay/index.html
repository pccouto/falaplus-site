Bilingual children and speech delay: do two languages hold a child back?

Many families speak two or more languages, and a common piece of advice is «use only one language so the child doesn’t get confused». Research and professional bodies say otherwise.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 4 min read
Loading reads…

Bilingualism and speech delay are often mentioned together, as if one caused the other. In this article we explain what research shows, how a child growing up with two languages at home should be assessed, and what families can do every day.

In short

  • Learning two or more languages does not cause a language disorder.
  • Vocabulary is spread across languages: counting only one underestimates what the child knows.
  • Mixing languages and a silent period in a new language are common.
  • Difficulties in every language call for an assessment, without blaming bilingualism.

What does research say about bilingualism?

Children can learn several languages from infancy. Professional bodies such as ASHA are clear: learning two languages does not cause a language disorder. A few observations help explain what is going on:

  • Spread-out vocabularyA child may know «apple» in one language and «maçã» in another. Counting only one language makes them seem to know less.
  • Mixing languagesUsing words from both languages in one sentence is part of the process, not a sign of confusion.
  • Silent periodWhen starting somewhere with a new language, such as nursery, a child may stay quiet for a while as they adjust.
  • Different pacesEach language can develop at its own pace, depending on how much and how well the child is exposed to it.

So can a bilingual child have difficulties?

Yes, just like a child growing up with one language. The difference is this: a language disorder shows up in all of the child’s languages, even if in different ways. If a difficulty appears in only one language, the most likely reason is less exposure to it.

That is why an assessment of a bilingual child should:

  • consider all of the child’s languages, and how long and with whom they hear each one;
  • count vocabulary across languages;
  • use an interpreter or a professional who knows the languages, when needed.

What we know

  • Bilingual children say their first words within the usual range, counting all languages.
  • Mixing languages is a normal phase.
  • Difficulties in both languages deserve an assessment.

Myths

  • «Two languages confuse children.»
  • «Use only one language so they talk sooner.»
  • «If they say little, it’s because they’re bilingual.»

How to support a bilingual child every day

  1. Each adult speaks the language they are most comfortable in, the one in which they can talk, play, sing and tell stories naturally.
  2. Keep the family language. It connects the child to grandparents, culture and part of their identity.
  3. Make contact with each language regular and enjoyable: conversations, books, songs and play.
  4. Don’t correct language mixing. Repeat the sentence with the right model, naturally.
  5. Tell the nursery or school which languages the child hears at home.
  6. If you are worried, ask for an assessment, without assuming bilingualism is the cause.

The activities to encourage speech work in any language. If you are worried, see also Speech delay: warning signs.

Frequently asked questions about bilingualism and speech

Do bilingual children start talking later?

They say their first words within the same range as other children, if you count the words in all their languages.

Is it bad for a child to mix languages?

No. It is a normal phase and shows they use every resource they have to communicate.

Should I stop speaking my language with the child?

Generally, no. It is better for each adult to speak the language in which they talk most naturally and richly.

The child has difficulties in both languages. Could it be bilingualism?

Bilingualism does not explain difficulties in every language. Talk to the child’s doctor or paediatrician and ask for an assessment.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
