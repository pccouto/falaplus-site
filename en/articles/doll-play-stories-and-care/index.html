Doll play — stories and everyday care

Use dolls to explore routines and stories without requiring speech or one correct way to play.

By the Talk+ team 27 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Doll play may begin with putting a bear to bed or taking it for a walk. A long story is unnecessary. Children can represent a routine through gestures, objects and small actions.

What does research tell us?

Quinn and colleagues’ meta-analysis found an association between symbolic play and language. Association does not prove causation. Lillard and colleagues’ critical review highlights limits to causal claims about pretend play. Neither source establishes the effectiveness of this particular toy for an individual child.

UNICEF and WHO’s Care for Child Development approach guides adults to observe and respond to children’s signals. In practice, offer an idea, allow time and adjust support. Commenting on what is happening can leave more room for initiative than asking for repeated answers.

Doll play: what can children explore?

A doll can represent someone eating, resting or wanting company. This creates space for sequences and different perspectives. Research on symbolic play and language does not show that caring for dolls teaches empathy or produces speech gains by itself.

How can we try this at home or in class?

  1. Offer a doll and two safe objects, such as a short blanket and a toy plate. Watch what the child does.
  2. Join the chosen routine: if the doll sleeps, lower your voice and comment “having a rest” without interrupting with questions.
  3. Offer a small change, such as choosing where the doll goes next. Accept a choice through gaze, gesture, picture or words.

How can play be adapted without pressure?

Choose characters the child enjoys without gender restrictions. Repeated routines can remain repeated. Add a second action or another participant only when this interests the child.

What material safety checks matter?

Check eyes, accessories and stuffing, and remove damaged dolls. Use large accessories and avoid cords or objects that could be swallowed.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • The activity creates opportunities to experiment, choose and communicate.
  • Suggestions can be adjusted to the child’s participation and interests.

What we cannot say

  • A toy, score or isolated play activity cannot assess development.
  • Using this material does not guarantee gains in speech, intelligence or school performance.

Frequently asked questions

Is doll play only for girls?

No. Characters and routines can interest any child. Gender should not determine the choice.

Must children give the doll a voice?

No. Rocking, pointing, moving or showing a picture can be part of the story. Speech is not a requirement to continue.

How can this be adapted for an autistic child?

Start with the individual child’s interests, sensory preferences and communication. Simplify materials, show what will happen and accept repetition, observation or a break. There is no single adaptation for all autistic children.

How long should the activity last?

There is no universal duration for this suggestion. Observe engagement, comfort and tiredness; stopping after a few attempts and returning another day is fine. Play is not a therapeutic dose.

Informational content and educational suggestions do not replace individual assessment or guidance. Performance in play or a game score is not a developmental assessment. Seek an appropriate health or education professional for persistent concerns.
