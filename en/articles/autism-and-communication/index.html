Autism and communication: understanding different ways to communicate

Communication is much more than talking. A child can communicate through words, gestures, facial expressions, movements, images or communication devices. In the case of autistic children, understanding these different forms of expression is essential to support language development and promote participation in everyday life.

Each autistic child has their own profile. Some develop fluent oral language, others use few words and others communicate mainly through alternative means. These differences do not, in themselves, determine the ability to understand, learn or establish relationships.

What is functional communication?

Functional communication consists of being able to transmit messages with a concrete objective: asking for something, refusing, making a choice, expressing discomfort, sharing interests or asking for help.

Imagine a child taking the adult to the refrigerator. Even without saying a word, you could be communicating that you want water or food.

Another example is a child who points to the door because he wants to leave. The gesture has a communicative intention and deserves to be recognized.

The first objective does not necessarily have to be to produce words. It could be helping the child to communicate in an increasingly autonomous and understandable way.

Echolalia: why do some children repeat the words they hear?

Echolalia is the repetition of previously heard words or phrases. It can take two main forms:

  • Immediate echolalia: the child repeats a word or expression immediately after hearing it.
  • Deferred echolalia: repetition happens later, for example, when the child uses a phrase from a cartoon during a game.

Echolalia should not be automatically interpreted as meaningless repetition. In certain situations, it can be used to ask, participate in an interaction, express an emotion or organize a response.

For example, a child who says a phrase associated with a snack may be communicating that he or she is hungry.

The meaning must be interpreted taking into account the context, and not just the words uttered.

Echolalia can also occur in typical development. When it is persistent or affects communication, a speech and language therapist can help to understand its role and identify appropriate strategies.

How to support the communication of an autistic child?

1. Observe before intervening.Notice situations in which the child looks for the adult, points, vocalizes or uses their own expressions.

2. Give meaning to communication attempts.If the child shows a glass, you can respond: «Do you want water? Let’s go get water.”

3. Use simple and natural sentences.Instead of asking lots of questions in a row, describe what is happening: «The car is moving», «The ball dropped», «Let's open».

4. Respect the response time. Some children need more time to process the information and organize a response.

5. Accept different forms of communication.A gesture, an image or a voice button can be equally valid responses.

6. Follow the child's interests.If you like trains, dinosaurs or music, these interests can become opportunities to communicate.

Can augmentative and alternative communication help?

Yes. Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) includes gestures, signs, images, communication boards and electronic devices that allow you to express messages.

It can complement speech or constitute the child's main form of communication.

The use of AAC does not impede the development of oral language. On the contrary, it can facilitate participation and reduce difficulties caused by the impossibility of communicating needs and preferences.

It is important that the choice and organisation of the system is adapted to the child and, whenever possible, supported by a speech and language therapist with relevant experience.

Conclusion

The development of communication in autistic children does not follow a single path. The priority must be to recognize the communicative intention, value different forms of expression and provide real opportunities for interaction.

Communicating is being understood, being able to choose and participate — with or without words.

If there are concerns about the development of communication, it is advisable to seek professional assessment without waiting for the difficulties to disappear spontaneously.

Informative content. It does not replace an individual assessment or guidance from a healthcare professional.

Frequently asked questions

Does an autistic child who doesn't speak communicate?

No. The child can communicate through gaze, movements, gestures, expressions, sounds, images or augmentative and alternative communication systems.

Does augmentative and alternative communication impede speech development?

There is no evidence that AAC impedes speech development. It can expand the possibilities of participation and communication.

Should I force the child to look into the eyes?

No. Attention and participation do not depend on constant eye contact. It is important to respect different forms of interaction.

When to seek professional support?

When there are persistent concerns about communication, participation or loss of skills, it is recommended to talk to qualified professionals.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
