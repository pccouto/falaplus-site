Language milestones from ages 2 to 5

Between the ages of two and five, children's communication goes through important changes. The first combinations of words give way to more complete sentences, conversations, questions and short stories.

Between the ages of two and five, children's communication goes through important changes. The first combinations of words give way to more complete sentences, conversations, questions and short stories.

Knowing developmental milestones helps families monitor these changes and recognize situations that deserve professional assessment.

However, milestones should not be used as a test that the child has to overcome. They are references to skills that most children already demonstrate at a certain age, not diagnostic tools.

At 2 years old: communicating needs and combining words

By the age of two, many children are able to:

  • Combine at least two words, such as “more water”.
  • Point to elements in a book when asked.
  • Identify at least two parts of the body.
  • Use different gestures to communicate, in addition to pointing or saying goodbye.

At this stage, it is important to observe not only how many words the child pronounces, but also how they understand others and how they try to communicate.

Suggested activity: during the game, use short phrases related to the objects: «Big ball», «Car moves», «More cookies».

Respond to the child's initiatives without requiring him to repeat every word.

At 3 years old: small conversations and questions

Around the age of three, skills such as:

  • Participate in a conversation with at least two message exchanges.
  • Asking questions like “Where?”, “Who?” or “Why?”.
  • Identify actions represented in images.
  • Say your first name when asked.
  • Speak in a way that is sufficiently understandable to others, in most situations.

Children also begin to use language to explain events, ask for clarification and share experiences.

Suggested activity: choose an illustrated book and ask «What is the boy doing?». Then, add information to the child's answer.

If you say “run”, you can answer: “Yes, the boy is running in the park.”

At 4 years old: longer sentences and events of the day

By the age of four, many children are able to:

  • Produce sentences with four or more words.
  • Say parts of songs, rhymes or well-known stories.
  • Tell at least one event from your day.
  • Answer simple questions about the use of objects, such as “What is a coat for?”.

Language becomes progressively more elaborate and the child begins to communicate experiences that are not happening at that moment.

Suggested activity: During dinner, talk about something that happened that day.

Questions like “Who did you play with?” or “What happened next?” can support the organization of ideas.

At 5 years old: telling stories and having conversations

Around the age of five, it is common to observe abilities such as:

  • Tell an invented or heard story with at least two events.
  • Answer questions about a story.
  • Maintain a conversation with more than three message exchanges.
  • Recognize or use simple rhymes.

At this stage, the child can also begin to better organize temporal sequences and use language more flexibly.

Suggested activity: use three images to build a short story. Help the child identify the beginning, development and end.

Summary of Language Milestones

AgeExamples of skills
2 yearsCombine two words, point and understand simple requests
3 yearsTalk, ask questions and describe actions
4 yearsProduce longer sentences and recount events
5 yearsTell short stories, chat and recognise rhymes

These examples are based on CDC developmental milestones, defined as skills demonstrated by at least 75% of children by the indicated age.

When to seek help?

It is advisable to talk to a healthcare professional or speech and language therapist when:

  • The child loses words or communicative skills that they already used.
  • There are persistent difficulties in understanding age-appropriate instructions.
  • Communication is too limited for everyday needs.
  • Speech is significantly difficult to understand.
  • The child presents difficulties that worry the family or educators.

A hearing assessment may also be important, because hearing difficulties can affect speech and language development.

There is no need to wait until a certain age to seek guidance.

How to support development on a daily basis?

The most useful activities do not always require specific materials.

Talking during meals, singing, sharing books, playing pretend and describing events creates natural opportunities for children to hear and use language.

Ideally, these activities should be accompanied by an adult and related to real experiences.

Conclusion

Language development does not happen at exactly the same pace in all children.

Knowing the milestones allows you to observe progress, identify possible difficulties and seek support in a timely manner, without turning each achievement into a competition.

More important than comparing children is understanding how each one communicates, learns and participates in the world.

Informative content. It does not replace an individual assessment or guidance from a healthcare professional.

Frequently asked questions

How many words should a 2 year old say?

Age references help with surveillance, but do not work as an isolated diagnosis. The quality of communication, understanding and evolution also matter.

Is it normal for each child to develop language at a different pace?

There is individual variation, but persistent differences, loss of skills or adult concern warrant professional guidance.

Do developmental milestones allow a diagnosis to be made?

No. They are surveillance references that help you decide when to talk to health and development professionals.

When should I seek help?

Seek guidance if there is a loss of skills, persistent difficulties in understanding or communicating, or any relevant concerns.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
