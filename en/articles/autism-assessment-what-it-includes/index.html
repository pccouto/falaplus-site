Autism assessment: what should be considered?

An autism diagnostic assessment is not just a checklist of behaviours. It aims to understand a child’s developmental history, communication and interaction, behaviour patterns and impact on everyday life.

By the Talk+ team 18 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

What sources may contribute?

The clinical team may speak with the family, observe the child, review health and developmental information and request examples from school or other settings. Depending on the question and profile, assessment may also consider language, hearing, cognition, independence, emotional health and co-occurring conditions.

Structured tools can organise observations, but they are interpreted by trained and experienced professionals. An isolated result does not replace diagnostic criteria or clinical judgement.

Why observe more than one situation?

A child may respond differently with different people, tasks or environments. If caregiver reports and clinic observations do not match, NICE guidance recommends considering additional information or observation in another setting. That difference is clinical information to understand, not proof that someone is exaggerating.

The process also considers other explanations and co-occurring conditions, such as language, hearing, attention, anxiety or broader developmental differences. Autism can coexist with other conditions.

What should be clear in the report?

Ask the report to explain the conclusion, the information supporting it, the assessment limits and the child’s strengths and support needs. Practical recommendations should be clear and linked to the child’s functioning, without reducing the person to a score.

If a diagnosis cannot be determined at that point, the report can state what information is missing, who will follow up and when to revisit the question. Necessary support can be considered without waiting for a definitive conclusion.

In short

  • Assessment integrates history, observation and information from different settings.
  • Standardised tools support clinical judgement, but none should be interpreted in isolation.

Conclusion

A useful assessment explains how the conclusion was reached and what the child needs now. Asking about limits, follow-up and support can make a report more helpful than seeking a diagnostic word alone.
