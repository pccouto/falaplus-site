How to support a child who cannot express what they want

A child may want something, refuse an activity or need help without being able to convey the message in a way others understand. When a child cannot express what they want, start by making communication more accessible, rather than demanding words or assuming what is causing the difficulty. Speech, gestures, pictures, signs and observable actions may offer clues, but check your interpretation with the child whenever possible.

> In brief: notice the context, reduce pressure, offer clear ways to choose or refuse and keep communication tools available. A gesture or point is communication too. Distress has no single cause and should not automatically be called defiance or lack of communication.

What might show that a child is trying to communicate?

A child may approach an object, move it away, lead an adult by the hand, vocalise, point, look between an object and a person, use a picture or repeat an action. Some signals are intentional and others are difficult to interpret. Context helps form a hypothesis, but does not confirm it.

For example, pushing a plate away might mean “no”, “finished”, “too hot” or simply moving an object. Rather than deciding immediately, pause and offer an accessible check: “Do you want to stop?” with a gesture, two pictures or a yes/no response. If there is no response, respecting the pause is better than insisting.

What can adults do when the message is unclear?

  1. Pause and observe. Notice what happened before, the environment, the activity and the child’s signals. Noise, tiredness, pain, a transition or difficulty understanding may matter; do not assume the cause is always communication.
  2. Use fewer words. A short phrase, gesture or picture may be easier to process than several questions in a row.
  3. Offer two genuine options. Show objects or pictures and accept pointing, touching, looking, approaching or a spoken word as a choice. Include “neither” or “no” when possible.
  4. Allow time. Wait without repeating the question several times. The child may need time to understand, decide and respond.
  5. Check without imposing. You might say “Are you asking for help?” and show the help symbol, while accepting a correction or no response.
  6. Keep messages available. Leave pictures, a communication board or device accessible even when the child is tired or distressed.

How can adults offer choices without pressure?

A choice should matter and allow the child to influence what happens. If an adult asks “blue coat or red coat?” after the decision has already been made, the choice may not be genuine. If neither option works, the child needs a way to say so.

At a meal, show two foods that are actually available. For a play activity, offer two objects or pictures. To request a break, show a gesture, card or “break” button. Do not make food, comfort, help or another basic need conditional on saying a particular word.

How should adults respond to distress?

When a child is very distressed, they may not be able to answer questions or learn a new communication method at that moment. Reducing demands and stimulation, keeping everyone safe and allowing space may be more useful than insisting on a choice. Communication can resume later when the child is ready.

Do not automatically assign a specific meaning to crying, movement or protest. Consider physical discomfort, pain, sensory overload, tiredness, changes in routine and other factors. If episodes are frequent, intense, new or raise safety or health concerns, talk with the professionals supporting the child.

The role of AAC

Augmentative and alternative communication includes unaided methods such as gestures and signs, and aided methods such as pictures, boards, writing or speech-generating devices. A child may use several methods depending on the person, place and need. AAC does not need to be limited to a “practice session”; it can be available at meals, during play, at school and elsewhere.

A speech and language therapist or AAC team can help choose vocabulary, symbols, access method and modelling strategies. Selection should consider the child’s vision, hearing, movement, language, preferences and communication settings. Meanwhile, family and educators can respond to the child’s current methods and avoid removing familiar tools.

Conclusion

Supporting communication starts with recognising that a child may have something to say even without words. Observing, offering understandable options, respecting refusals and keeping AAC available are general steps that can help adults respond. Individual assessment can clarify which methods and supports are most suitable.

Frequently asked questions

What if I do not understand what the child wants?

Pause, observe the context, use simple language and offer accessible options. Check your guess without insisting, and accept that the message may not be clear straight away.

Does a child need to say a word to get what they asked for?

Basic needs and help should not be conditional on speech. Respond to available communication and model words without pressure.

Does challenging behaviour mean a child wants something?

Not necessarily. There can be many reasons, including pain or tiredness. Observe the context and seek professional advice when concerned.

Do pictures replace speech?

Pictures may complement communication and offer another way to express ideas. They can be used alongside speech, gestures and other methods.

When should I seek advice?

If communication difficulties limit choices, participation or wellbeing, or if skills are lost or there are health concerns, speak with qualified professionals.
