Speech delay or autism? The differences that matter

A 2-year-old who says only a few words leads many families to wonder about autism. Talking late, on its own, does not answer that question. What helps most is looking at how the child communicates with other people.

By the Talk+ team 24 October 2026 5 min read
Loading reads…

«Speech delay or autism?» is one of the most searched questions by people caring for young children. In this article we explain what research says about late talkers, which social communication signs help tell the two apart and why it is best not to wait before asking for help.

In short

  • About 13% of 24-month-olds are late to start talking; most of them are not autistic.
  • In autism, the key difference lies in social communication: sharing attention, pointing to show, responding to their name.
  • A child who says little but uses gestures, looks at you and seeks you out has a different profile.
  • Standardised autism screening is recommended at 18 and 24 months, and support should start as soon as there is a concern, without waiting for a diagnosis.

How many children are late talkers?

In an Australian study of 1,766 children, about 13% of 24-month-olds met the criterion for late language emergence (Zubrick et al., 2007). The factors linked to it were a family history of late talking, being a boy and some early growth factors. Parental education and socioeconomic level did not increase the risk.

Most of these children are not autistic. But autism also often starts with a concern about speech, so the question makes sense. See also Do late talkers always catch up?.

Late talker vs autism: what to look for

The key concept is social communication: the drive and the way a child shares with others. A child who is simply a late talker usually makes up for it with other ways of communicating. The signs below are tendencies, not rules:

BehaviourMore typical of a late talkerMay warrant an autism assessment
PointingPoints to ask and to show («look!»)Points little or only to ask; leads the adult’s hand to the object
Shared attentionLooks at the object, then at the adult, to shareRarely shifts gaze between the object and the person
Responding to nameTurns when calledOften does not respond to their name, even though they can hear
Gestures and expressionsUses gestures, waves, pulls faces to communicateUses few gestures and expressions aimed at others
PlayPlays simple pretend games and imitatesPlays repetitively; imitates little
Losing skillsDoes not lose words or gesturesLoses words or social skills they already had

None of these signs, on its own, allows a diagnosis. Autism is diagnosed by professionals using structured observation and interviews. Learn more in Does a speech delay mean autism? and what an autism assessment includes.

Talk to the child’s doctor without waiting if the child:

  • has lost words, gestures or interest in people, at any age;
  • at 12 months does not point, wave or often respond to their name;
  • at 18 months does not point to show you things that interest them;
  • at 24 months does not combine two meaningful words (repeating does not count).

Why is screening at 18 and 24 months recommended?

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends standardised autism screening at 18 and 24 months, alongside developmental surveillance at every visit (Hyman et al., 2020). A widely used tool is the M-CHAT-R/F, a parent questionnaire followed by an interview.

The same report recommends that children be referred for intervention as soon as a concern is identified, without waiting for a formal diagnosis. This applies in both scenarios: if it turns out to be only a speech delay, support helps too. A positive screen is not a diagnosis, and a negative one does not rule autism out: see what a positive screen means.

What we know

  • Many late talkers are not autistic.
  • Social communication is what best separates the two profiles.
  • Starting support early is recommended, with or without a diagnosis.

Myths

  • «If they talk little, it’s autism.»
  • «If they say a few words, it can’t be autism.»
  • «Better to wait until age 3 and see.»

What to do at home while you wait

  • Follow the child’s interest and comment on what they are doing, in short sentences.
  • Build small pauses into play so the child can ask for «more» with a word, a gesture or a look.
  • Value every way of communicating: pointing, gestures, sounds and pictures.
  • Write down concrete examples (what the child does, with whom, in which situation) to bring to the appointment.

Frequently asked questions about speech delay and autism

Is a speech delay a sign of autism?

Not necessarily. Many late talkers are not autistic. What helps most to tell them apart is social communication: pointing to show, sharing attention and responding to their name.

My 2-year-old is not talking but understands and interacts. Could it be autism?

If the child seeks you out, uses gestures and shares what they see, the profile is more typical of a late talker. Still, talk to the doctor: a delay at age 2 deserves an assessment.

At what age can autism be diagnosed?

In many cases autism can be diagnosed from 18 months. Standardised screening is recommended at 18 and 24 months.

Should I wait and see if speech comes?

It is not the best option. The recommendation is to start support as soon as there is a concern, without waiting for a diagnosis.

Can an autistic child talk?

Yes. Profiles vary widely: some autistic children talk a lot, others little or not at all, and many benefit from augmentative communication.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
