Learning to read at age 6: games with a purpose

Learning to read games at age 6 should build on what the child understands and what school is teaching. Age helps frame examples; it does not put every child at the same stage.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • Games aligned with teaching can provide meaningful reading practice.
  • An app cannot guarantee literacy, and a game score cannot identify dyslexia.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Reading involves connecting print with speech sounds and understanding a message. The IES guide addresses sound awareness, links to letters, decoding and connected text. Memorising word shapes or guessing from pictures does not replace learning how print works.

Ask the teacher which letter–sound relationships have been taught. Choose words containing these and offer brief help. Reciting a familiar book from memory and reading an unfamiliar word are different tasks. Notice both without turning play into an examination. English spelling also requires explicit teaching of patterns and exceptions.

References for this background: IES / What Works Clearinghouse — Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding · IES / REL Southeast — Supporting Your Child’s Reading at Home.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • Find the wordWrite three short words using taught correspondences. Read each by blending sounds, with help when needed. Then find its object or picture.
  • A message for a toyUse a short sentence such as “The cat is on the mat”, if its patterns are familiar. Read it and act it out. Change one word and discuss what changes.
  • A tiny adventure bookFold paper into two scenes. Help write short captions. The child reads an accessible part and listens to the rest, then talks about the beginning and ending.

Making the activity accessible

Use a few words, large print and no timer. A child using AAC can choose pictures or order elements to show understanding. Demonstrate unfamiliar steps first. An available adult can support digital play, but adult participation is a recommendation rather than a condition for playing.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Discuss persistent difficulty with the teacher, especially when suitable instruction and support have not helped or reading causes distress. Consider language, hearing and vision concerns. Judge progress in the context of learning opportunities rather than an age deadline.

Frequently asked questions

Must every six-year-old read independently?

No individual conclusion follows from age alone. School entry, instruction and support needs matter.

Should we start with letters or words?

Connect sounds and letters within accessible words, following school teaching and discussing meaning.

Can we use pictures?

Yes, for discussion and checking meaning. Avoid using them to guess instead of reading the print.

How long should we play?

Try a brief round and finish before fatigue. Helpful interaction matters more than accumulating minutes.
