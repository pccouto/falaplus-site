Autism medication: risperidone, aripiprazole and speech

No medicine treats autism or makes a child talk. Some medicines treat problems that can come with autism, such as severe irritability, anxiety or sleep difficulties, and can make daily life much easier when well prescribed.

Autism medication raises many questions. Here we explain what risperidone and aripiprazole were studied for, what monitoring they need and what guidelines say about the core features of autism.

In short

  • No medicine is approved for the core features of autism (social communication, restricted interests) or for language.
  • In the United States, risperidone and aripiprazole are FDA-approved for irritability associated with autism (aggression, self-injury, severe meltdowns).
  • NICE (UK) advises against antipsychotics for core features and only to consider them when psychosocial support is not enough.
  • They need monitoring of weight, appetite, sleep and blood tests, and regular review.

Is there a medication for autism?

No. Autism is a different way of developing, not an illness that a medicine corrects. NICE guidance in the UK explicitly says not to use medicines (antipsychotics, antidepressants or anticonvulsants) to manage the core features of autism. Medication can, however, treat associated problems that cause distress.

What are risperidone and aripiprazole used for?

In a randomised trial of 101 autistic children with severe tantrums, aggression or self-injury, risperidone reduced irritability far more than placebo (McCracken et al., 2002). On the basis of studies like this, the FDA approved risperidone for irritability associated with autism from 5 to 16 years and aripiprazole from 6 to 17 years. In other countries, including the UK, approved uses differ; the prescribing doctor will explain what applies where the child lives.

When meltdowns ease, a child may be more available to learn and communicate. That is an indirect effect: neither medicine has been shown to treat speech or language.

Which side effects should be watched?

  • Increased appetite and weight gain (very common, especially with risperidone).
  • Sleepiness or tiredness.
  • Hormonal and metabolic changes (for example prolactin, glucose, cholesterol), monitored with blood tests.
  • Involuntary movements, less often.

Never stop or change the dose on your own. Contact the doctor if the child:

  • gains a lot of weight quickly;
  • has unusual movements, stiffness or excessive sleepiness;
  • seems to be in pain or discomfort they cannot explain.

What we know

  • Risperidone and aripiprazole reduce severe irritability in many autistic children.
  • They should come after or alongside psychosocial support, not instead of it.
  • They need regular monitoring.

What cannot be said

  • «There is a pill that cures autism.»
  • «Medication makes the child talk.»
  • «Once it works, it is for life.» The need should be reviewed.

How can a child communicate side effects and discomfort?

Many autistic children find it hard to say that something hurts or feels wrong. Giving them a way to show it (pictures, gestures, cards) helps the family and doctor notice side effects and other causes of distress, such as pain.

Frequently asked questions about autism medication

Is there a medicine for autism?

There is no medicine for autism itself. There are medicines for associated problems such as severe irritability, anxiety or sleep.

Does risperidone help an autistic child talk?

That has not been shown. It can reduce severe meltdowns, which may leave the child more available to communicate.

Does risperidone cause weight gain?

Increased appetite and weight gain are among the most common side effects and should be monitored.

How long is medication taken for?

It depends on the child. Guidelines call for regular review of the need and the dose.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
