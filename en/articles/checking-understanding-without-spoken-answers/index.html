Checking understanding without spoken answers

Checking understanding without spoken answers makes room for different ways of showing knowledge. Saying “yes” to “did you understand?” does not always explain what was understood.

Checking understanding without spoken answers makes room for different ways of showing knowledge. Saying “yes” to “did you understand?” does not always explain what was understood.

Checking understanding without spoken answers: what is known?

Explicit teaching includes checking understanding during learning, rather than only at the end. EEF guidance highlights clear explanations, examples and adjustments based on what pupils show. An incorrect response should inform the next explanation. (Education Endowment Foundation)

ASHA describes multiple communication modalities and the importance of access. NAEYC highlights differences between assessment contexts. Not speaking, being unable to point or not responding to an unfamiliar adult does not automatically mean lack of understanding. (ASHA) (NAEYC)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Define the target: understanding “inside”, recognising a character or following one step. Do not try to assess everything at once.
  2. Present information with accessible language and an example. Make sure materials can be seen, reached and recognised.
  3. Invite a target-related response: put a figure inside a box or select the picture of an event. Avoid adding an unnecessary speech demand.
  4. Change option positions and offer a new example. This helps distinguish a content-related choice from a preference for one side.
  5. If difficulty arises, explain differently and offer another opportunity. Record the help given rather than presenting the taught answer as independent work.

What might this look like in a real situation?

To explore “bigger”, an adult offers two containers and asks the child to choose the bigger one. Another pair follows, in different colours. Always choosing the same colour suggests something to revisit in teaching, rather than an immediate conclusion about ability.

How can access be adapted?

Agree how the child responds: touch, gesture, gaze interpreted by a skilled partner, AAC selection or an object action. Check response consistency rather than guessing intentions. Supporting the material may provide access; pointing to the correct answer changes what can be concluded.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Separate the request, the response and the help given. “Selected twice after a demonstration” is more useful than “knows it now”. In standardised tests, accommodations and interpretation belong with the professional and may limit comparison with norms.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Knowledge can be demonstrated without an oral answer.
  • Errors and inconsistent responses can help adults adjust teaching.

What we cannot say

  • One choice between two options proves complete understanding.
  • No response demonstrates low intelligence or disobedience.

Frequently asked questions

How can understanding be explored in a child who does not speak?

Observe actions and choices across situations using an accessible response method. Seek a professional assessment of language that considers the whole profile.

Does pointing to an answer show understanding?

It may provide useful information when pointing is intentional and the task is clear. A single choice may also reflect chance or other cues.

Should I always ask the child to repeat an instruction?

No. Repeating words may not show meaning, and an oral demand can create a barrier. Invite an action or choice related to the goal.

Can I adapt a clinical test at home?

Do not turn these suggestions into a home version of a test. Instruments require trained administration and interpretation under appropriate conditions.
