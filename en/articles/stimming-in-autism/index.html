Stimming: why does a child flap their hands?

Flapping hands when happy, rocking, spinning, repeating a sound. These movements are called stereotypies and, in the autistic community, “stimming”. They are common, often serve a purpose and do not always need to be stopped.

Stereotypies are repetitive movements or sounds, such as hand flapping, rocking, jumping or repeating words. They are one of the features described in autism, but they also appear in typically developing children. In this article we explain what is known about stimming and how to tell what to accept from what deserves support.

In short

  • Repetitive movements are common in childhood and are not enough, on their own, for a diagnosis.
  • In autism, stimming can help regulate emotions, cope with sensory input or express joy.
  • Autistic adults describe stimming as helpful and ask that it not be suppressed without reason.
  • Stepping in only makes sense when there is harm, risk or a major impact on participation.

What are stereotypies?

  • MotorFlapping hands, rocking, spinning, jumping, moving fingers in front of the eyes.
  • VocalRepeating sounds, words or phrases, humming.
  • With objectsSpinning objects, lining up toys, opening and closing doors.
  • SensorySmelling, touching textures, seeking pressure or movement.

These behaviours are part of the diagnostic criteria for autism, along with intense interests and a preference for routines, but they are only clinically meaningful when they appear together with differences in communication and social interaction (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Many young children without autism flap their hands when excited.

What is stimming for?

In a study with autistic adults, participants described stimming as a way to soothe anxiety, cope with too much sensory input, concentrate and express emotions, including joy. Many reported that being stopped from stimming as children was distressing (Kapp and colleagues, 2019).

So noticing when stimming appears helps you understand what the child is feeling: excitement, tiredness, noise, waiting, frustration.

When does it make sense to step in?

May deserve support

  • When the child gets hurt (head banging, biting themselves).
  • When there is a safety risk.
  • When the behaviour seriously prevents learning or taking part.

Usually does not need stopping

  • Flapping hands with joy.
  • Rocking to calm down.
  • Repeating favourite sounds or phrases.
  • Look for the cause: is the environment very noisy? Is the child waiting without knowing for what?
  • Reduce sensory input and prepare for changes with visual supports (see Visual routines).
  • If stimming is dangerous, work with the therapist on an alternative that serves the same purpose, rather than banning it.
  • If there is no assessment yet and there are other signs, talk to a doctor (see Speech delay or autism?).

Frequently asked questions about stimming

My toddler flaps their hands when happy. Is it autism?

Not necessarily. It is common in young children. It only becomes meaningful alongside differences in communication and social interaction.

Should I stop stimming?

Generally, no. Stepping in only makes sense when there is harm, risk or a major impact on participation.

Do stereotypies go away with age?

Some lessen, others change form. Many autistic people keep some forms of stimming throughout life.

Are stereotypies and tics the same thing?

No. Tics are quick, involuntary movements; stereotypies tend to be more rhythmic and linked to emotional state. A doctor can tell them apart.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
