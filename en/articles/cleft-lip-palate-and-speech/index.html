Cleft lip and palate and speech: what to expect

Cleft lip and palate is one of the most common birth differences. With surgery at the right time and support from a team, many children develop clear speech; some need more support along the way.

By the Talk+ team 24 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

The link between cleft lip and palate and speech depends mainly on the palate (the roof of the mouth). In this article we explain why a cleft palate can affect speech, what velopharyngeal insufficiency is, why hearing should be monitored and how the team works.

In short

  • Cleft lip and palate affects about 1 in 700 births.
  • When the palate does not fully close off the nose during speech, the voice can sound hypernasal.
  • Children with a cleft palate have more glue ear, so hearing should be monitored.
  • Speech therapy is part of the care, but some cases also need surgery.

Why can a cleft palate affect speech?

For most sounds, the soft palate rises and closes the passage between the mouth and the nose. If that closure is incomplete, air escapes through the nose: the voice sounds hypernasal and some sounds (such as p, t, s) become weak. This is called velopharyngeal insufficiency.

A cleft lip on its own usually has less impact on speech than a cleft palate.

What does the team do?

  • SurgeryThe palate is closed in the first months of life, following the team’s protocol.
  • HearingGlue ear is common in these children; see Glue ear and speech.
  • Speech therapyFollows the development of sounds and language and helps correct compensatory patterns.
  • ReassessmentIf the voice stays hypernasal, the team assesses velopharyngeal closure.

An umbrella review of velopharyngeal insufficiency concluded that for children with persistent symptoms, treatment did not consist of speech therapy alone: surgery was often needed, with no single technique considered the gold standard (umbrella review, 2022).

Talk to the team if the child:

  • has a very nasal voice or air escaping through the nose when talking;
  • seems not to hear well or has frequent ear infections;
  • makes many sound errors or is hard to understand for their age;
  • has feeding difficulties, such as liquids coming out of the nose.

What we know

  • Many children with a cleft develop clear speech.
  • Team follow-up and hearing monitoring are essential.
  • Some cases need surgery in addition to speech therapy.

What we cannot say

  • «After surgery, speech is always normal.»
  • «Speech therapy always fixes hypernasal speech.»
  • «Blowing exercises correct the palate.»

How to support communication at home

  • Talk, read and sing with the child as with any other: language develops through interaction.
  • Value what they want to say; do not demand they repeat «correct» sounds.
  • Follow the speech and language therapist’s guidance on the sounds being worked on.
  • Be wary of blowing exercises as a solution: see do facial exercises speed up speech?.

Frequently asked questions about cleft palate and speech

Will a child with a cleft lip and palate talk normally?

Many develop clear speech with surgery and follow-up. Some need more support or other interventions.

Why does the voice sound nasal?

Because the palate does not fully close the passage to the nose during speech (velopharyngeal insufficiency).

Does speech therapy fix hypernasal speech?

It depends on the cause. When velopharyngeal closure is insufficient, surgery is often needed.

Why does hearing need monitoring?

Children with a cleft palate have more glue ear, which can lower hearing.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
