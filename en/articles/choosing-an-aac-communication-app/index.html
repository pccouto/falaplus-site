How to choose a communication app for a child’s needs

A communication app may include pictures, words, voice or other ways to build messages. By the Talk+ team 15 October 2026 5 min read

A communication app may include pictures, words, voice or other ways to build messages. Choosing an AAC app is not just about selecting the most colourful interface: consider whether the child can access the vocabulary they need, understand the symbols and use the system in settings where they communicate.

> In brief: consider the child, family and school; try access, vocabulary, flexibility, sound and reliability; read the privacy policy; and involve AAC-experienced professionals. No app suits everyone, and a child should not be left without communication while a family searches for a “perfect” option.

What should you consider first?

Start with everyday communication needs. Does the child need to request, refuse, comment, call someone, talk about feelings, join lessons or communicate in emergencies? An app limited to requesting objects may not support all the messages the child wants to convey.

Consider what makes access possible: vision, hearing, movement, ability to touch or point, symbol size, number of visible choices, language and familiarity with the device. These factors are not reasons to exclude a child; they help adapt the tool and access method.

Practical criteria for comparing AAC apps

1. Useful and varied vocabulary

Check whether the app supports more than requests: “no”, “help”, “stop”, “like”, questions, comments, people, places and words relevant to the child’s life. Vocabulary should be meaningful and able to grow with the child.

2. Clear, consistent access

Notice whether the child can find and select symbols using the screen, buttons or another available method. Large icons, predictable organisation, contrast and adjustable layouts may matter. Try it in real situations without pressuring the child to use it.

3. Personalisation

Check whether you can add words, photos, languages or familiar voices. Personalisation may make references more recognisable, while a consistent layout helps the child and communication partners find vocabulary.

4. Voice and other output

If the app has speech output, listen to whether it is clear in the intended setting and whether the child controls when a message is spoken. Check if they can also communicate silently by showing a sentence on screen. A device should not be the child’s only way to be heard.

5. Availability and reliability

Consider battery, offline use, updates, device compatibility and what happens if the device fails. A backup method, such as cards or a printed board, may be useful when communication cannot depend on one device.

6. Privacy and data

Read what data the app collects, whether it records voice, stores photos or shares information. Check permissions, accounts, ads, purchases and data deletion options. Photos and voice recordings are personal information; obtain appropriate consent and limit access.

7. Communication partners

Family, school and professionals may need to learn how to model the system and respond to messages. Ask whether the interface lets an adult demonstrate words without requiring the child to repeat them. An app is one tool; access and responsive communication partners matter too.

How can you try an app without pressure?

Explore the system during activities the child chooses. An adult can use a few symbols while speaking to show how the app works, without turning each screen into a quiz. Accept responses through gestures, speech, pictures or movement. If the system is hard to navigate or the child does not want to use it at that moment, adjust the context rather than concluding they are “not ready”.

An individual assessment by a speech and language therapist or AAC team may help choose symbols, access and vocabulary. Family know the child’s routines and preferences; professionals contribute technical experience. The choice can be reviewed as needs change.

AAC apps and spoken language

AAC may supplement or replace spoken language as needed. It should not be removed to force speech. Available evidence does not support the idea that access to AAC prevents speech; outcomes vary and specific effects should not be promised for an app. The immediate purpose is to ensure a functional way to communicate.

    Conclusion

    Choose a communication app for how it supports the child, not for effects or promises. Checking vocabulary, access, personalisation, voice, privacy and alternatives supports an informed decision. Keep the system available without requiring speech, and review the choice with the child and their team.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the best AAC app?

    There is no single best option for every child. Choice depends on access, vocabulary, languages, settings, preferences and privacy.

    Is an app with pictures enough for AAC?

    It may support some messages, but vocabulary coverage and flexibility differ. Consider the full range of messages the child needs.

    Should I choose an app that works offline?

    Offline access can help where there is no connection or the network fails. Also compare battery, safety, accessibility and backup options.

    Can I use photos and recordings of the child?

    First understand how the app stores and handles the data and limit access. Check its privacy policy and permissions.

    Does an app replace a speech and language therapist?

    No. An app is a tool; it does not assess needs or replace professional collaboration when needed.

    This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
