Selective mutism: understanding and supporting a child

Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder in which a child speaks in some settings but is consistently unable to speak in others where speaking is expected. It is not a choice, defiance or rudeness. It can affect participation at school and with others, so understanding and assessment matter.

Why might a child speak at home but not at school?

The setting, people present and activity can affect a child’s ability to speak. A child may communicate through gestures, facial expressions, writing or pictures, or remain silent. The pattern varies and cannot be identified from one situation alone.

A child learning a new language may speak less while becoming familiar with it. That period alone does not confirm selective mutism. Assessment considers the languages the child knows, the settings where they communicate, and any other speech, language, hearing or anxiety needs.

How can adults support the child without adding pressure?

  • Do not demand speech or put the child on the spot in public.
  • Accept responses through gestures, pointing, writing or other means.
  • Agree with school on predictable ways to participate without drawing attention to the child.
  • Share observations between family, school and the professionals supporting the child.
  • Seek advice when persistent silence affects communication, learning or wellbeing.

Support is usually planned by professionals with experience in childhood anxiety and communication, working with family and school. An article cannot diagnose a child.

Frequently asked questions

Is selective mutism a choice?

No. A child is not simply deciding not to speak; anxiety can make speaking very difficult in certain settings.

Are selective mutism and autism the same?

No. They are distinct conditions, though they can co-occur. A comprehensive assessment is needed to understand a child’s needs.

Does selective mutism go away?

A particular outcome should not be promised. Support is individualised and aims to make communication and participation easier while respecting the child’s pace.

A child does not speak at school. What should I do?

Avoid pressure and speak with the school and health professionals. Note when the child communicates and which forms of expression they use.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
