When a child speaks little or is difficult to understand, people may use terms such as “speech delay” and “developmental language disorder” (DLD). They do not necessarily mean the same thing: speech delay is a broad description of a concern, while DLD is a clinical term considered after an assessment. Speech, language and communication are related, but describe different skills.

> In brief: speaking few words, finding speech sounds difficult and struggling to understand sentences are different concerns. An individual assessment considers understanding, expression, speech, communication in context and developmental history. A checklist or app cannot confirm a diagnosis.

What does “speech delay” mean?

“Speech delay” is often used informally when a child’s spoken communication seems to be developing later than expected. It is not, by itself, a diagnosis. The concern may actually relate to language: for example, a small vocabulary, difficulty combining words or difficulty understanding what people say.

  • Speech is how sounds are produced, including articulation, voice and fluency.
  • Language involves understanding and expressing words, sentences and meanings through speech, writing, sign or other means.
  • Communication is the exchange of messages and intentions, which can include speech, gestures, facial expression, pictures and devices.

A child may communicate through gestures while using few spoken words. Another child may talk often but have difficulty understanding instructions or organising sentences. These differences help guide an assessment, but do not identify a condition on their own.

What is developmental language disorder?

DLD describes persistent language difficulties that affect communication and everyday life. It can affect understanding, expression or both. Profiles vary: a child may find vocabulary, sentence structure, instructions, storytelling or language in social situations difficult.

The CATALISE consensus uses “developmental language disorder” for language difficulties with functional impact when there is no differentiating biomedical condition. When a condition such as autism or a genetic condition is associated, clinicians may describe language difficulties as associated with that condition. This does not mean every child with the same condition has the same language profile.

DLD is not the same as difficulty pronouncing a sound. It does not mean a child lacks intelligence, effort or conversation at home. Children can have language support needs alongside many strengths.

How is DLD different from a speech difficulty?

A speech assessment may focus on the sounds a child produces, how consistently they produce them and how easy they are to understand. A language assessment considers what a child understands and how they communicate ideas, combine words, learn vocabulary and take part in conversations.

These areas can co-occur. A child may find speech sounds difficult and also have trouble building sentences. It is therefore not enough to ask whether they “say words correctly”. Communication at home, school and in other settings, including any non-speaking communication, also matters.

How is language assessed?

A speech and language therapist may consider the child’s age, languages, hearing, learning opportunities and everyday communication. Assessment can include a family discussion, play and interaction, age-appropriate activities and information from school. The format depends on the child and service.

If a child uses more than one language, the assessment should consider all of them and the child’s opportunities to use each one. Looking at only one language can provide an incomplete picture. Being exposed to two languages does not cause DLD; when a language difficulty is present, it may appear across a child’s languages in different ways.

A professional may recommend a hearing assessment or involve other professionals when relevant. Concerns do not need to wait for a medical diagnosis or a “wait and see” period before being discussed. Seeking advice does not commit a family to a diagnosis; it helps clarify needs and next steps.

How can families and educators support communication?

  1. Follow the child’s interest. Talk about what they are looking at or doing.
  2. Use clear, natural sentences. Comment rather than turning every moment into a quiz.
  3. Allow time to respond. A pause gives the child a chance to process and participate.
  4. Accept all communication. A gesture, picture, sound or word can carry an important message.
  5. Expand without demanding repetition. If the child says “car”, you might say “Yes, the red car is here.”
  6. Share examples with the team. Everyday situations can show what helps or makes communication harder.

These are general strategies, not an individual therapy plan or a guarantee of a particular result.

Conclusion

“Speech delay” describes a concern but does not explain what is happening. DLD is one possibility that requires an assessment of language and everyday impact. When concerns persist, professional advice can help identify the child’s strengths and needs and plan support without waiting for a label.

Frequently asked questions

Are speech delay and DLD the same?

No. Speech delay is a broad description of concern; DLD is a clinical term for persistent language difficulties with functional impact, considered after assessment.

Can a child with DLD understand well?

Profiles differ. Some children have more difficulty expressing themselves; others also have difficulty understanding. Both need to be considered.

Is DLD the same as mispronouncing sounds?

No. Sound production is a speech area; DLD concerns language. They can co-occur but are not interchangeable.

Does bilingualism cause DLD?

No. Exposure to two or more languages does not cause a language disorder. Assessment should consider the child’s language skills across all their languages.

Who can assess a child’s language?

A speech and language therapist can assess speech, language and communication and work with other professionals when needed. A doctor or paediatrician can also help families access appropriate care.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
