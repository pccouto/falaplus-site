Reading stories to boost language: dialogic reading

Reading aloud is good. Reading so that the child asks, answers and tells the story too is even better for vocabulary. That is the idea behind dialogic reading.

By the Talk+ team 24 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Reading stories to boost language is one of the most common recommendations to families. In this article we explain what dialogic reading is, what meta-analyses show about shared book reading and how to put it into practice in a few minutes a day.

In short

  • In dialogic reading, the adult asks questions, waits, praises and expands the child’s answers.
  • One meta-analysis found a moderate effect on expressive vocabulary compared with ordinary reading.
  • Another meta-analysis of 19 trials found small effects on language and larger effects on adults’ skills.
  • Dose matters: very short programmes had little effect.

What is dialogic reading?

It is a way of reading in which the child goes from listener to storyteller. It was described by Whitehurst and colleagues (1988). The adult uses a simple sequence known as PEER:

  • Prompt«What’s this?», «What will happen next?»
  • EvaluatePraise and, if needed, help: «Well done, it’s a dog!»
  • ExpandAdd information: «It’s a brown dog, running.»
  • RepeatInvite the child to say it again: «You say it: a dog running.»

Does reading stories help a child talk? What studies say

A meta-analysis of 16 studies compared dialogic reading with ordinary reading and found a moderate effect on expressive vocabulary. The effect was smaller for 5- and 6-year-olds and for children at risk of difficulties (Mol et al., 2008).

A more recent meta-analysis of 19 randomised trials with 2,594 children aged 1 to 6 found small effects on expressive and receptive language and a large effect on the adult’s book-sharing skills. Programmes with very low doses had minimal effect (Dowdall et al., 2020).

What we know

  • Reading with the child taking part helps vocabulary.
  • Adults learn the technique quickly.
  • Regularity matters.

What we cannot say

  • «Reading stories fixes a speech delay.»
  • «Reading is only worth it from age 3.»
  • «Audiobooks alone are enough.»

How to do it at home, age by age

AgeIdeas
1 to 2 yearsBoard books with big pictures; point and name; wait for the child to point too.
2 to 3 yearsSimple questions («Where’s the cat?»), finishing repeated phrases from the story.
3 to 5 yearsOpen questions («Why is he sad?»), predicting what comes next, retelling the story.
  • Follow the child’s interest: you can stay on one page for a long time.
  • Reread favourite books: repetition helps.
  • A few minutes every day beats an hour now and then.

Frequently asked questions about reading stories

Does reading stories help a child talk?

Yes, especially when the child takes part: questions, pauses and answers. Studies show effects on vocabulary.

When should I start reading to my baby?

Early on. With babies, what matters is looking at pictures together, naming things and responding to their sounds.

How long should I read each day?

There is no magic number. Reading a little every day, regularly, is most useful.

What if my child won’t sit for a story?

Choose books about their interests, let them turn the pages and turn reading into a chat about the pictures.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
