Do vaccines cause autism? Myths and evidence

What scientific reviews show, and how to distinguish timing from cause.

By the Talk+ team 18 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

What scientific reviews show, and how to distinguish timing from cause.

The myth

“Vaccines cause autism.”

What the evidence says

Available evidence does not show a causal relationship between vaccines and autism. In December 2025, the WHO Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety reviewed studies published from 2010 to 2025 and reaffirmed that conclusion. Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with multiple contributing factors; it is not caused by parenting choices or a vaccine.

What we can say

  • Available evidence does not show a causal relationship between vaccines and autism. In December 2025, the WHO Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety reviewed studies published from 2010 to 2025 and reaffirmed that conclusion. Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with multiple contributing factors; it is not caused by parenting choices or a vaccine.

What we cannot say

  • “Vaccines cause autism.”

What to do day to day

Discuss vaccine questions with the doctor or nurse who knows the child. If you notice developmental differences or a loss of skills, write down examples and seek an assessment; do not wait for one explanation or attribute a change to a vaccine without clinical evaluation.

Myth or fact: how to read the evidence

A study can show that two things occur together without showing that one caused the other. Look for research reviews, professional guidance and individual assessment; consider the population studied, limitations and source date.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do vaccines cause autism? Myths and evidence

    Available evidence does not show a causal relationship between vaccines and autism. In December 2025, the WHO Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety reviewed studies published from 2010 to 2025 and reaffirmed that conclusion. Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with multiple contributing factors; it is not caused by parenting choices or a vaccine.

    Does an association mean that one thing caused the other?

    Not necessarily. Observational studies can identify relationships but do not establish cause on their own. Study design, other factors and the full body of evidence matter.

    When should I seek advice?

    If a concern persists, affects participation or skills are lost, speak with the child’s doctor or paediatrician and ask what assessment may be appropriate.

    What can I do next?

    Discuss vaccine questions with the doctor or nurse who knows the child. If you notice developmental differences or a loss of skills, write down examples and seek an assessment; do not wait for one explanation or attribute a change to a vaccine without clinical evaluation.
