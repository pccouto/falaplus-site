Phonological awareness: rhymes, syllables and sounds before reading

Before learning to read, a child needs to realise that words are made of pieces: syllables and sounds that can be counted, swapped and joined. This ability to “play” with the sounds of language is called phonological awareness.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Phonological awareness is one of the most studied foundations of learning to read. In this article we explain its levels, what is expected at each age and suggest simple phonological awareness activities with rhymes, syllables and sounds for home or the classroom.

In short

  • Phonological awareness means noticing and manipulating the sounds in words, without looking at letters.
  • It develops from larger units (words, syllables, rhymes) to smaller ones (sounds).
  • It is one of the best predictors of reading, and it can be practised with short games.
  • Children with speech delay may need extra support in this area.

The levels of phonological awareness

LevelWhat the child doesExample
WordsNotices that a sentence has several words.“The cat sleeps” has three words.
SyllablesSplits and blends syllables.Clapping: ba-na-na.
RhymesRecognises and makes rhymes.“Cat” rhymes with “hat”.
First soundsIdentifies the first sound.“Moon” and “mum” start with /m/.
PhonemesSeparates, blends and swaps sounds./s/ /u/ /n/ makes “sun”.

Around 3–4 years, many children can already split words into syllables and enjoy rhymes; at 5–6 years, they start identifying first sounds. Manipulating individual sounds usually consolidates with formal reading instruction. Each child has their own pace.

Why does it matter so much?

A meta-analysis showed that phonemic awareness is one of the strongest predictors of reading (Melby-Lervåg, Lyster and Hulme, 2012), and another concluded that teaching it explicitly improves reading, especially in short sessions linked to letters (Ehri and colleagues, 2001). See also Speech delay, reading and dyslexia.

Simple games to play at home

  • Syllable clapsSay the child’s and family members’ names with one clap per syllable. Whose name is longest?
  • Rhyme huntRead a nursery rhyme and pause before the rhyming word for the child to finish.
  • Sound bagCollect objects that start with the same sound (mug, map, mitten) and find the odd one out.
  • Robot wordsSay a word slowly, in pieces (“ra… bbit”), and the child guesses.
  • I spyOut and about: “I spy something beginning with /b/”.
  • Play for a few minutes at a time, playfully, and stop before the child gets tired.
  • Use letter sounds, not letter names: /m/, not “em”.
  • If the child has speech difficulties, start with syllables and rhymes and ask a speech and language therapist for guidance.

Frequently asked questions about phonological awareness

What is phonological awareness?

The ability to notice and manipulate the sounds in words: counting syllables, recognising rhymes, identifying and blending sounds.

At what age can you start?

Rhyme and syllable games can start around age 3, always playfully and without pressure.

Are phonological and phonemic awareness the same?

No. Phonemic awareness is the finest level, of individual sounds; phonological awareness also includes words, syllables and rhymes.

My child cannot rhyme at 5. Should I worry?

It may be worth talking to the teacher or a speech and language therapist, especially if there are also speech difficulties.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
