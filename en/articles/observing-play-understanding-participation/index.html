Observing play to understand participation

Observing play helps adults understand how a child chooses, explores, communicates and participates. Describing events can be more useful than a broad impression such as “cannot concentrate”.

Observing play helps adults understand how a child chooses, explores, communicates and participates. Describing events can be more useful than a broad impression such as “cannot concentrate”.

Observing play: what is known?

NAEYC recommends ongoing observation, documentation and information from different activities to guide teaching. Children may show different skills with different materials, people and settings. One situation is a sample, not a complete picture. (NAEYC)

ASHA includes observation, caregiver information and language sampling among methods that may complement other instruments. Participation in routines is relevant in early intervention. A family observation does not thereby become a standalone clinical assessment. (ASHA) (ASHA)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Choose a specific question: how does the child ask for help when a piece will not fit? Avoid trying to observe every developmental area at once.
  2. Offer familiar, safe materials and accessible communication. Do not deliberately create frustration to provoke a response.
  3. Write observable actions: “moved the box towards the adult”, “said help” or “pushed the toy away”. Separate these from interpretations such as “was being manipulative”.
  4. Record who was there, prompts, noise, fatigue and familiarity with materials. Support belongs in the description.
  5. Share examples from more than one occasion with family, school and professionals. Use them to choose a specific adjustment and observe again.

What might this look like in a real situation?

Illustrative note: “On the floor with cars, selected a vehicle by pointing. When a wheel became stuck, moved it towards the adult. Nodded after the adult asked whether help was wanted. Pushed the box away at the end”. This records choices and communication, not a diagnosis or intelligence score.

How can access be adapted?

For low vision or motor difficulty, describe access and the partner’s role. For a multilingual child, record the language and familiarity with the communication partner. Lack of speech, limited eye contact or parallel play should not individually become conclusions about inability to participate.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

A simple record can include date, context, action, communication and help. Keep only what is needed and share with those supporting the child rather than publishing identifiable videos or data. Seek professional advice for skill loss, persistent distress or developmental concerns without waiting for a perfect record.

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • Repeated, contextualised descriptions can guide adjustments and professional discussion.
  • The same child may participate differently when the environment changes.

What we cannot say

  • An informal checklist diagnoses autism, ADHD or global developmental delay.
  • Observing participation means scoring compliance, eye contact or sitting still.

Frequently asked questions

What should adults observe during play?

Choices, actions, communication, barriers and support. Start with a specific question rather than a diagnostic checklist.

How should I write a child observation?

Describe what you saw and heard, include context and support, and label interpretations as hypotheses. Avoid assigning motives.

Does play-based assessment remove the need for tests?

Not necessarily. Professionals select methods for the question and integrate history, observations and suitable instruments.

What if a skill appears at home but not in the clinic?

Both sources matter. Share examples and ask how context, access and familiarity will inform interpretation.
