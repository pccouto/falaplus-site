Does the child understand what is said? Receptive language explained

When people talk about speech delay, attention almost always goes to the words a child says. But there is another equally important question: how much does the child understand? Understanding comes before talking and is one of the best indicators of what to expect.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read

Receptive language is the ability to understand words, sentences and instructions. Expressive language is what the child says or communicates. In this article we explain the difference, what is expected by age and how to support understanding every day. See also Speech, language and communication.

In short

  • Understanding develops before speech: children understand many more words than they say.
  • A child who talks little but understands well generally has a better outlook.
  • Difficulties understanding are a reason to ask for an assessment without waiting.
  • Short sentences, gestures, pictures and routines help a child understand.

What is expected at each age?

Approximate ageMany children can
12 monthsRespond to their name, understand “no” and some familiar words, such as “mummy” or “water”.
18 monthsFollow simple instructions with a gesture (“give me the ball”), point to body parts or pictures.
2 yearsFollow two-step related instructions (“get your shoes and bring them to me”), understand simple questions.
3 yearsUnderstand “on”, “in”, “big/little”, and questions with “who”, “where” and “what”.
4–5 yearsUnderstand simple stories, multi-part instructions and “why” questions.

These are general guides; every child has their own pace. See also Language milestones from 2 to 5.

Why does understanding matter so much?

Among children who start talking late, those with difficulties understanding are more likely to have persistent difficulties than those who understand well (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, Late Language Emergence). The international CATALISE consensus includes comprehension among the factors that help decide when to assess and intervene (Bishop and colleagues, 2017).

Signs of difficulty understanding

  • Seems not to hear or ignores people talking to them, yet hears other sounds well.
  • Only follows instructions with gestures or when the routine is always the same.
  • Repeats the question instead of answering (see Echolalia).
  • Answers “yes” to everything or gives answers that do not match the question.
  • Gets lost in stories or group conversations.

Before concluding it is a language difficulty, hearing problems need to be ruled out (see Hearing tests for children).

How to support understanding at home

  • Get the child’s attention before you speak: say their name and wait for them to look.
  • Use short sentences and one instruction at a time.
  • Add gestures, objects or pictures to what you say.
  • Talk about what is happening right now, in the here and now.
  • Give the child time to process before repeating.
  • Use routines and visual supports to show what comes next (see Visual routines).

Frequently asked questions about receptive language

What is receptive language?

The ability to understand words, sentences and instructions. Expressive language is the ability to use them.

The child understands everything but does not talk. Is that a concern?

Understanding well is a good sign, but a speech delay deserves assessment. See the article on speech delay.

How do I know whether the child understands or is just following the routine?

Try a simple instruction outside the usual context and without gestures, for example asking for an object that is out of sight.

Who assesses understanding?

A speech and language therapist, usually after a doctor’s referral and once hearing has been checked.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
