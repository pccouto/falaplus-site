Autism level 1, 2 or 3: what do the levels of support mean?

Many diagnostic reports say “autism spectrum disorder, level 1” or “level 2”. Families immediately ask whether that is mild or severe, and what it says about the future. The answer is simpler, and more reassuring, than it seems.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

The autism levels of support were introduced in DSM-5 to describe how much support a person needs, at a given time, in two areas: social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviours. In this article we explain what autism level 1, 2 or 3 means, the limits of these levels and how they relate to ICD-11.

In short

  • The level describes the intensity of support needed, not intelligence or a person’s worth.
  • It is given separately for social communication and for repetitive behaviours.
  • It can change over time, with development, support and context.
  • ICD-11 does not use levels: it records whether there is intellectual disability and the degree of functional language.

What does each level say?

LevelDSM-5 labelIn general terms
Level 1Requiring supportWithout support, differences in communication and flexibility cause noticeable difficulties day to day.
Level 2Requiring substantial supportMarked differences, noticeable even with support, that limit participation in several situations.
Level 3Requiring very substantial supportVery marked differences in communication and great difficulty coping with change, affecting almost every area.

A report may, for example, state level 2 for social communication and level 1 for repetitive behaviours. It should also note whether there is associated intellectual impairment and language impairment (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). See also Autism criteria: how to read the report.

What the levels do not say

The levels say

  • How intense the support needs to be now.
  • In which area support is most needed.
  • A reference for planning support.

The levels do not say

  • “Level 1 is mild autism with no needs.”
  • “The level is permanent.”
  • “The level predicts the child’s intelligence or future.”

One study showed that level assignment varies considerably and does not always match the severity measures used in assessment, reinforcing that levels are a clinical description, not an exact measurement (Weitlauf and colleagues, 2014). A child at “level 1” may have significant needs, for example around anxiety or learning.

What about ICD-11?

The International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), used in many health systems, codes autism spectrum disorder as 6A02 and describes two dimensions: whether there is a disorder of intellectual development and the degree of functional language. It does not use levels 1, 2 and 3 (see What diagnosis codes in a report mean).

Useful questions for the appointment

  • In which specific situations does the child need more support?
  • What support do you recommend at school and at home?
  • When should we reassess?
  • What strengths did you observe?

Frequently asked questions about autism levels

Is level 1 autism mild autism?

Not exactly. It means the person needs support, but their needs can still be significant, especially in demanding situations.

Can an autism level change?

Yes. It describes needs at one point in time and can change with development, support and context.

What is the difference between level 2 and level 3?

Level 3 corresponds to a need for very substantial support, affecting almost every area; level 2 to substantial support.

Why does the report have no level?

Some professionals use ICD-11, which has no levels, or prefer to describe needs in detail.
