Speech delay at 4 and 5: when it is no longer “just a delay”

At 4 and 5, language becomes the main tool for learning, playing with friends and saying how you feel. If a child still talks little, with disorganised or hard-to-follow sentences, it is wise to act before primary school starts.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

A speech delay at 4 or 5 is different from a delay at 2. At this age, difficulties that persist are more likely to continue and may reflect a developmental language disorder (DLD) or a speech sound disorder. In this article we explain what is expected and which steps to take. See also Speech delay: warning signs.

In short

  • At 4–5, most children tell stories, explain, ask complex questions and are understood by everyone.
  • Difficulties that persist after 4–5 are more likely to continue at school.
  • DLD affects about 7 in 100 children and often goes unnoticed.
  • An assessment makes it possible to plan therapy and support at nursery and school.

What is expected at 4 and 5?

  • TellingSays what happened or tells a short story with a beginning, middle and end.
  • SentencesUses long sentences with “because”, “when”, “but”.
  • ClarityIs understood by strangers; a few sound errors, such as /r/ or “th”, may remain.
  • UnderstandingFollows multi-part instructions and answers questions about stories.

These guidelines are based on milestones revised by the American Academy of Pediatrics (Zubler and colleagues, 2022). See also Language milestones from 2 to 5.

Why is this age different?

The international CATALISE consensus notes that after age 5, language difficulties that affect daily life are more likely to persist, and proposes the term DLD when they are not linked to another known condition (Bishop and colleagues, 2017). In a population study in England, about 7.6% of children starting school had DLD (Norbury and colleagues, 2016). See Speech delay and DLD.

Ask for an assessment if, at 4–5, the child

  • uses short sentences or leaves out many words;
  • struggles to say what happened;
  • is hard to understand for people who do not know them;
  • seems not to understand instructions or stories;
  • avoids talking to other children or adults.

Steps to take before school

  1. Ask the doctor for a hearing assessment and a speech and language therapy referral.
  2. Share your concern with nursery staff and ask them to note what they observe.
  3. Ask the school what learning support measures are available.
  4. Plan the move to primary school with family, school and therapists (see School transitions).

Frequently asked questions about speech delay at 4 and 5

The 4-year-old does not talk well. Is that normal?

Some sound errors can be normal, but very short sentences, difficulty telling things or hard-to-understand speech need assessment.

Can a speech delay at 5 still catch up?

With support, many children make good progress. But difficulties at this age rarely disappear without intervention.

What is DLD?

Developmental language disorder: persistent language difficulties that affect daily life and are not explained by another condition.

Can school help?

Yes. Schools can put learning support in place, including adjustments and specialist support.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
