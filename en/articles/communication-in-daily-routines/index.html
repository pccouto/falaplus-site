How to make everyday routines opportunities for communication

Bath time, meals, getting dressed and outings are already part of the day. There is no need to create a lesson or ask a child to repeat words: routines can offer natural opportunities to communicate, choose, refuse, comment or ask for help.

By the Talk+ team 15 October 2026 5 min read

Communication in daily routines does not require special materials or extra activities. Research on responsive caregiving highlights noticing a child’s signals, following their interest and responding appropriately. A familiar routine can give words, gestures, pictures and other forms of expression a meaningful context.

In short

  • Choose a moment that already happens and follow the child’s interest.
  • Use simple language, pause and accept different ways of responding.
  • Do not make food, help or access to an object depend on saying a word.
  • If the interaction creates tension, simplify or stop; the day does not need to become an exercise.

What makes a routine a moment for communication?

A routine creates an opportunity when people share attention and can exchange messages. An adult can name an action, offer a genuine choice, imitate a play action or comment on what the child is doing. The child may respond with a look, movement, gesture, picture, sound or word.

The goal is not to get a particular response. If the child is tired, uncomfortable or does not want to join in, respect the pause. The routine should still serve its purpose and should not depend on the child saying a word.

How can adults support communication in six routines?

Bath time

Comment on concrete actions such as “water”, “towel” or “wash hands”. Show two bath toys for the child to choose from, or accept moving one away to indicate “no”. If it is safe and comfortable, playing with water and cups can also create turns.

Meals

Name the foods on the table and allow the child to choose between available options. They may ask for more, refuse, show they are finished or ask for help with a gesture or picture. Respond to communication without making food or drink conditional on a spoken request.

Getting dressed

Offer two suitable items when there is a real choice. Use useful words such as “sock”, “shoe”, “more” or “help”. If a texture or item causes discomfort, do not assume the child is being uncooperative: try to understand what is happening and adapt the routine with the family or support team.

Play

Follow the child’s choice. Stacking blocks, rolling a ball, building a road or pretend cooking can create turns, requests and comments. Leave natural pauses for the child to initiate, but do not withhold a wanted object for a long time to force a request.

Outings

Before leaving, explain what will happen and offer a simple choice if one is available. During the outing, comment on what catches the child’s attention: “the bus went by” or “can you hear the bird?”. A photograph or symbol may help show what is coming next, but it does not replace supervision or safety precautions.

Bedtime

A book, song or familiar sequence can create a calm moment to share attention. The child may choose a story or indicate “more”, “finished” or “lights out”. If they are very tired, shorten the interaction; do not extend bedtime to complete an activity.

How can augmentative and alternative communication be included?

If the child uses gestures, signs, pictures or a device, keep those methods available in settings where they are useful. An adult can point to a picture while speaking without requiring the child to do the same. Augmentative and alternative communication can support a range of messages: asking, refusing, commenting, calling someone or expressing feelings.

The choice of symbols and devices should consider the child and the people they communicate with. A speech and language therapist or AAC team can advise. Meanwhile, accepting the child’s current ways of communicating means that speech is not the only recognised method.

How can you tell whether a routine is working?

Notice whether the child has opportunities to initiate, choose or take part; do not count only words. Also consider whether the activity remains enjoyable and manageable for the family. If a strategy takes too much preparation or creates tension, simplify or stop. There is no need to turn every moment into an exercise.

Frequently asked questions about communication in daily routines

Do I have to talk all day to encourage communication?

There is no required daily amount. Attentive interactions connected to what the child is doing, with pauses for them to take part, may be more useful than filling every silence.

How can I encourage communication at mealtimes?

Name foods, offer genuine choices and respond to gestures, pictures, sounds or words. Do not make food or drink conditional on a spoken request.

What if the child does not respond?

They may be busy, tired, processing information or simply not want to join in. Wait, simplify or continue the routine without insisting.

Do routines replace speech and language therapy?

No. Everyday routines may complement professional guidance, but they do not replace assessment or therapy when needed.

Do I need to use pictures for every routine?

No. Use pictures or other supports when they are useful and understandable to the child. They are not required for every person or situation.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
