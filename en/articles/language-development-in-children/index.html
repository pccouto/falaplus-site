Speech and language · Guide

Language development in children: what to know and how to help

When do first words appear? Is it normal to understand everything but say little? We explain how children learn to communicate, what adults can do every day and when it makes sense to seek an assessment.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 6 min read

Language development in children is one of the topics that raises the most questions for the adults around them. In this guide we explain, simply and based on research, how speech, language and communication develop, and what really helps.

In short

  • Speech, language and communication are different things, and a child can communicate long before talking.
  • Understanding usually comes before speaking, but every profile is unique.
  • What helps most are responsive everyday interactions: following the child’s interest, responding and giving time.
  • If you are worried, the best step is to talk to a professional, without waiting.

Speech, language and communication: are they the same?

No, and the difference helps make sense of many situations:

  • CommunicationSharing and understanding intentions: looking, pointing, gestures, sounds, words, signs or pictures.
  • LanguageThe system of words and rules: vocabulary, grammar, sentences, telling what happened. It can be spoken, written or signed.
  • SpeechProducing sounds: articulation, voice, rhythm and fluency.
  • In practiceA child may communicate well with gestures before saying words, or have a large vocabulary and mix up some sounds.

Does understanding come before talking?

In most children, receptive language (understanding) runs ahead of expressive language (saying). Many children follow simple instructions, recognise the names of objects and people and react to questions before they can answer in words.

Even so, every profile is different. Understanding well does not rule out a language difficulty, and saying little does not mean understanding little. That is why an assessment looks at both sides, not just the number of words.

When do babies and toddlers start talking?

Children vary a great deal. Language development milestones are used to monitor, not to diagnose. In most countries, a child’s development is checked at routine health visits with the family doctor, paediatrician or health visitor.

One reference used in research: at around 2, a child is often considered a late talker when they use fewer than about 50 words or do not yet put two words together. It is a guide for deciding when to talk to a professional, not a diagnosis.

The signs worth watching for are explained in Speech delay: warning signs and when to seek help.

What helps language development?

Research highlights responsive interactions: moments when the adult pays attention to what interests the child and responds to what they communicate. Some simple practices:

  1. Follow the child’s interest. Talk about what they are looking at or doing, rather than changing the subject.
  2. Respond to every form of communication. A gesture, a sound or a look deserves a response, just like a word.
  3. Give time. Waiting a few seconds after a comment gives the child space to join in.
  4. Expand rather than correct. If the child says «car», the adult can reply «the blue car is fast!».
  5. Take turns. «My turn, your turn» games create conversations even before words.
  6. Read and talk about books. Point, name, ask «what next?» and let the child turn the pages.

These practices describe responsive ways of interacting; they are not a therapy programme and do not guarantee a result. You will find concrete ideas in Activities to encourage speech.

What role does play have?

Play offers a natural setting for communicating: naming objects, asking, commenting, negotiating and inventing stories. A meta-analysis of 35 studies found an association between pretend play and language. An association does not prove that pretend play, on its own, moves language forward: it shows the two tend to go together. See The importance of play and Pretend play.

Does growing up with two languages delay speech?

No. Learning two or more languages does not cause a language disorder. A child’s vocabulary may be spread across languages, so counting words in only one language can underestimate what they know. An assessment should consider all of the child’s languages.

What if the child says little or does not talk?

Some children communicate mainly through gestures, signs, pictures or devices. Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is a valid way to communicate and, according to the available research, does not stop speech from developing. We explain how it works in Augmentative and alternative communication.

Myths to set aside

What we know

  • A concern deserves to be discussed with a professional, without waiting.
  • Understanding and expression can be at very different levels.
  • Language difficulties have complex causes.

Myths

  • «They’ll talk when they’re ready.»
  • «If they don’t talk, they don’t understand.»
  • «It’s the parents’ fault» or «it’s because of two languages at home».

Frequently asked questions about language development

What is the difference between receptive and expressive language?

Receptive language is the ability to understand what is heard or seen; expressive language is the ability to share ideas through words, gestures, signs or pictures.

How many words should a 2-year-old say?

There is no single number for every child. As a reference, research considers a child of around 2 to be a late talker when they use fewer than 50 words or do not yet combine two words. It is a monitoring guide, not a diagnosis: if in doubt, talk to the family doctor or paediatrician.

Do screens delay speech?

A difficulty should not be blamed on a single cause. What matters is the content, the time, whether an adult is involved and whether the screen takes the place of sleep, movement and conversation.

Who should I talk to if I am worried?

The child’s doctor or paediatrician, who can arrange a hearing test and a referral to a speech and language therapist. Losing words or skills the child already had should be assessed quickly.

Can a list of signs on the internet diagnose a child?

No. No list, article or app makes a diagnosis. It only helps you decide when to talk to a professional.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
