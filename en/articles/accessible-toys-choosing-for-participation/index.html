Accessible toys: choosing for participation

Accessible toys let a child take part in a way that interests them. A diagnosis and an age label alone cannot determine a good match.

Accessible toys let a child take part in a way that interests them. A diagnosis and an age label alone cannot determine a good match.

Accessible toys: what is known?

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends materials matched to children’s abilities and interests and values interaction around toys. An expensive product or an “educational” label does not automatically make a better choice. (American Academy of Pediatrics)

Access involves reaching, manipulating, seeing, hearing and influencing play. ASHA considers motor, sensory and communication factors when selecting AAC resources; this perspective helps adults ask access questions without making every toy a therapeutic device. (ASHA)

How can this be explored in practice?

These are examples to adapt to the child and context, not a validated programme. For assessment, they help prepare discussion with professionals rather than teach test administration or scoring.

  1. Notice what the child seeks: movement, construction, caring for dolls or exploring pictures. Begin with an actual interest rather than a supposedly diagnosis-appropriate activity.
  2. Check whether the toy can be reached, held and activated without an uncomfortable posture. Consider large, stable pieces and a suitable non-slip surface.
  3. Check that the child can choose, refuse and request help. Keep their established communication system available alongside gestures and words.
  4. Compare simple options: knob puzzles, easy-grip blocks or an instrument responding to light touch. Try before buying when possible.
  5. Choose for observed participation and safety rather than the number of features. Seek competent professional advice for electrical adaptations or specialist access selection.

What might this look like in a real situation?

Two children may enjoy the same puzzle with different access. One handles the pieces; another indicates a piece and its location for a partner to place it. Both can make decisions and participate. If direct handling is a separate goal, work on it without devaluing participation through choice.

How can access be adapted?

For 2- and 3-year-olds, and older children who mouth objects, size and detachable pieces need particular attention. Follow safety age warnings. Do not offer an unsafe version because it appears to match a learning level. Materials should respect interests and the dignity of the child’s actual age.

What should be noticed and shared with the team?

Notice whether the child can start an action, choose to continue and finish. Pain, rapid fatigue or postural effort are reasons to review access. Hospital safety guidance highlights small parts, batteries and magnets; do not leave button batteries, loose magnets or damaged components accessible. (UCSF Benioff Children’s Hospitals)

What can and cannot be concluded?

What we can say

  • A simple toy can allow choices, exploration and interaction.
  • Suitable access may involve an adaptation or another person’s cooperation.

What we cannot say

  • One toy is ideal for every child with the same diagnosis.
  • A therapeutic label guarantees clinical benefits or higher intelligence.

Frequently asked questions

Which toys suit an autistic child?

Start with interests and sensory comfort, check access and safety, and explore without demanding one correct way to play.

What are the best toys for speech delay?

No toy list treats speech delay. Objects that invite choosing, commenting and sharing may create communication opportunities with an available adult.

Can a baby toy suit an older child?

Consider safety, interests and actual age. Look for a similar operating principle in an age-respectful presentation rather than reducing the child to a developmental age.

Can an app replace physical toys?

No. Objects, movement and people have their own place in play. A digital resource can be complementary when accessible and meaningful.
