Music and songs to support language development

A song repeats the same words, has a predictable rhythm and invites gestures. No wonder so many children say their first words while finishing a nursery rhyme. Music is a natural ally of language.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

Singing with a child is simple, free and fun. In this article we explain what research says about music and language development in childhood and share ideas for using songs to encourage speech at home, in the car or at nursery. For clinical work with music, see Music therapy for autism.

In short

  • Songs combine repetition, rhythm and rhyme, which help children notice and remember words.
  • In the preschool years, perception of rhythm and melody is linked to better language skills.
  • In babies, active music classes with parents were linked to more communicative gestures.
  • What matters most is singing together, with pauses for the child to join in.

What do studies show?

In a study with 3- to 6-year-olds, musical perception, especially of rhythm and melody, and exposure to music at home were associated with better vocabulary, grammar and phonological awareness (Politimou and colleagues, 2019).

In another study, babies who took part with their parents in active music classes (singing, moving, playing) showed, after six months, more early communicative gestures, such as pointing and waving, than babies who only listened to music (Gerry, Unrau and Trainor, 2012).

These are association studies or small trials: they show that music is a good companion to language, not that it replaces conversation or therapy.

Why do songs work?

  • RepetitionThe same words come back again and again without becoming boring.
  • RhythmHelps separate words and syllables in the flow of speech.
  • RhymeDraws attention to the sounds of words, the basis of phonological awareness.
  • GesturesMany songs have actions the child can do before they can say the words.
  • PredictabilityThe child knows what comes next and can anticipate and fill in.

Practical ideas

  • Sing slowly and pause before a familiar word for the child to fill in (“Twinkle, twinkle, little…”).
  • Use songs in routines: washing hands, tidying up, bath time.
  • Swap words in the song for the child’s name or nearby objects.
  • Add simple actions and repeat them the same way every time.
  • Play together: a saucepan drum, rice shakers, clapping.
  • Prefer live singing to constant background music.

Frequently asked questions about music and language

Does singing help a child talk?

Songs offer repetition, rhythm and gestures that support language, especially when you sing together with the child.

Does playing music all day help?

Not as much. What counts is singing and playing with the child, with pauses for them to join in.

Which songs are best?

Short, repetitive songs with actions, in the family’s language. The child’s favourites are great.

Is music the same as music therapy?

No. Music therapy is an intervention delivered by a music therapist, with defined goals.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
