Psychomotor therapy in childhood: goals and assessment

“Psychomotor therapy” can refer to different practices depending on country, profession and service. Before starting, clarify who provides it, which method they use, what skill is assessed and what evidence supports the child’s specific goal.

What may be addressed?

Depending on the provider’s training and plan, activities may involve balance, coordination, motor planning, body awareness, play and task participation. These goals may also fall within physiotherapy or occupational therapy, so ask how the chosen approach differs and coordinates with other services.

How can families judge whether it is appropriate?

Ask for observable functional goals, such as dressing, joining play or completing a specific motor task, and agree how changes will be measured. International recommendations for developmental coordination disorder emphasise task-oriented interventions and participation. Do not assume a generic psychomotor practice produces these outcomes; monitor benefit, effort and the child’s preferences.

In short

  • Goals depend on individual needs, preferences and assessment.
  • The team should explain the plan and review its effects on participation and wellbeing.

For information only. It does not replace individual assessment or clinical advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is psychomotor therapy the same profession everywhere?

No. The title and regulation vary; check training, professional regulation and local scope.

Can it replace physiotherapy or occupational therapy?

Not automatically. The team should clarify goals and avoid duplication or gaps in support.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.

Sources

  2. Blank et al. (2019), International clinical practice recommendations for developmental coordination disorder.
