ABA therapy for autism: what it is, what the evidence says and the current debate

ABA is one of the interventions families of autistic children search for most and, at the same time, one of the most debated. Between promises of results and criticism from autistic people, it is worth understanding what is actually known.

By the Talk+ team 25 October 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

ABA therapy (applied behaviour analysis) uses learning principles, such as reinforcement, to teach skills and reduce behaviours that cause difficulties. In this article we explain the different formats of ABA for autism, what scientific reviews say and why the topic is debated.

In short

  • ABA is not a single method: it ranges from highly structured programmes to naturalistic, play-based approaches.
  • The latest reviews find low to moderate quality evidence and less clear effects than previously thought.
  • Naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions, which blend ABA with play, show more consistent results for social communication.
  • Autistic people criticise programmes focused on compliance or on suppressing harmless behaviours.

Which formats exist?

  • Discrete trial trainingStructured, table-top sessions with short instructions, responses and reinforcement.
  • Early intensive behavioural interventionMany hours a week at an early age, based on discrete trials.
  • Naturalistic developmental behavioural interventionsTeaching within play and routines, starting from the child’s interests (for example the Early Start Denver Model or JASPER).
  • Positive behaviour supportUnderstanding what a behaviour is for and changing the environment (see Positive behaviour support).

What does the evidence say?

A large meta-analysis of interventions for young autistic children concluded that, when only the highest-quality studies are considered (randomised, with assessors unaware of the child’s group), the effects of classic behavioural interventions become unclear, while naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions and developmental approaches show more consistent effects on social communication (Sandbank and colleagues, 2020; updated in 2023).

Another review showed that most intervention studies do not record possible adverse effects, which makes it hard to weigh risks (Bottema-Beutel and colleagues, 2021). This does not mean ABA does not work; it means the evidence is less solid than is often claimed.

Why is ABA debated?

Many autistic adults report that programmes centred on “looking less autistic”, on compliance or on stopping harmless behaviours, such as stimming, were harmful (see Stimming). Professionals and researchers have been calling for programmes that respect the child’s autonomy, assent and wellbeing.

Questions to ask before choosing a programme

  • What specific goals do you propose, and why do they matter in the child’s daily life?
  • Do the goals include communication, play and independence, or mainly compliance?
  • How can the child say “no” or “break” during sessions?
  • How many hours do you propose, and on what basis?
  • How do you measure progress and wellbeing, and what do you do if the child shows distress?
  • Does the family take part and learn strategies for home?

Frequently asked questions about ABA therapy

Does ABA therapy work?

There is evidence of some benefits, but the most rigorous reviews show less clear effects than previously thought. Results vary a lot by type of programme.

Is ABA the only proven treatment for autism?

No. Naturalistic developmental interventions, parent coaching and speech and language therapy also have evidence.

How many hours of ABA are needed?

There is no proven number. More hours do not necessarily mean better outcomes.

Why do some autistic people criticise ABA?

Because of experiences with programmes focused on compliance and on suppressing harmless behaviours, without attention to wellbeing.
