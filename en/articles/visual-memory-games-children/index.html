Visual memory games for children: look and remember

Visual memory games ask children to remember an object, position or sequence they have seen. Spotting differences while both pictures remain visible is visual comparison, a different demand from recalling a hidden picture.

By the Talk+ team 9 November 2026 3 min read
Loading reads…

In short

  • A game can offer practice recalling the particular elements and positions presented.
  • Scores do not measure intelligence, diagnose visual difficulties or guarantee school improvement.
  • Adapt the task, accept different ways of responding and consider context before interpreting a result.

The basis for these suggestions

Somerset’s children’s therapy service includes visual memory among perceptual skills and suggests recall and matching games. Performance also depends on seeing the materials clearly, understanding them and attending. Difficulty on a board does not describe the child’s entire memory.

This guide distinguishes observation, recalling a missing item and remembering positions. These are adaptable examples, not standardised tests. Improvement with a familiar set may reflect learning its pieces and rules; it does not itself demonstrate gains in reading or general memory.

References for this background: Somerset NHS Foundation Trust — Visual perceptual skills · Humber Teaching NHS Foundation Trust — Auditory Processing and Memory Games.

Three ideas to try

The activities below are educational examples created for this article, informed by the source principles. They are not a validated intervention programme. Choose one, demonstrate it and allow the child to try, ask for help, take a break or stop.

  • The missing objectShow three large familiar objects and name them if helpful. Cover them, remove one and find what is missing. Offer two visible choices if needed.
  • Where was it?Use two pairs of simple drawings. Look at positions before turning cards over and find one pair at a time. Add cards only if the child wants and can follow.
  • A tiny constructionMake a sequence of two distinguishable coloured blocks. Observe, hide and rebuild it. Start with the model visible if needed, explaining that this changes the task.

Making the activity accessible

Provide contrast, large pieces and an uncluttered surface. Add shape or texture when colours are hard to distinguish. Pointing or selecting can replace naming. Seek individual access advice for low vision; a tactile task is not an equivalent visual memory test.

Notice what made participation easier: fewer items, a demonstration or a gesture response. Return to that version before increasing difficulty. Compare with the child’s own previous experience rather than ranking children.

When to seek advice

Discuss persistent difficulties finding materials, copying or recognising elements with school and relevant professionals. If reduced vision is suspected, arrange a vision assessment rather than assume more memory practice is needed.

What we can conclude

What we can say

  • A game can offer practice recalling the particular elements and positions presented.

What we cannot say

  • Scores do not measure intelligence, diagnose visual difficulties or guarantee school improvement.

Frequently asked questions

Is spotting differences visual memory?

It may involve memory, but simultaneous pictures mainly require comparison and discrimination.

Must the child name each object?

No. Pointing or selecting allows a choice without an additional speech demand.

Are more cards always better?

No. A smaller set may enable strategies and enjoyable participation.

Will games improve memory everywhere?

Performance on one task does not automatically demonstrate transfer to other contexts.
