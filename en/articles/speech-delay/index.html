Speech delay: warning signs and when to seek help

«They’re nearly two and say very few words. Is it a speech delay?» Some children start talking later and catch up; others need support. Find out what to look for and when to talk to a professional.

By the Talk+ team 14 October 2026 5 min read

Speech delay is one of the biggest worries for adults caring for young children. The honest answer is that it depends on many factors. In this article we explain what «speech delay» means, which warning signs are worth raising with a professional and which myths to set aside.

In short

  • «Speech delay» can mean different things: sounds, words, understanding or communication.
  • Many late talkers catch up, but nobody can know for sure who will.
  • If you are worried, the way forward is to assess and monitor, not to «wait and see».
  • Losing words or skills the child already had calls for a prompt assessment.

What exactly is a «speech delay»?

In everyday use, «speech delay» is used for almost everything. Professionals prefer to say exactly what is affected:

  • SpeechProducing sounds: the child swaps or drops sounds and is hard to understand.
  • Expressive languageFew words or short sentences for their age.
  • Receptive languageDifficulty understanding what is said to them.
  • Social communicationUses gestures, eye gaze or sharing interest only a little.

The distinction matters because each situation has different explanations and support. A child may have more than one of these difficulties.

Is talking late always a problem?

No. Many children who start talking late do catch up. But nobody can know for sure, at a single point in time, who will catch up and who will need support. That is why the recommended approach is to assess and monitor.

We also know that when language difficulties persist beyond the early years, they tend to stay with the child at school. A 2026 Cochrane review found this association with language and reading over time, which supports not delaying an assessment.

Speech delay in a 2-year-old: what signs to watch for?

In research, a child of around 2 is often considered a late talker when they use fewer than 50 words or do not yet put two words together («more juice», «daddy gone»). Other signs call for attention, especially when they appear together:

  • the child understands little of what is said to them;
  • uses few gestures, such as pointing, waving or showing;
  • shows little interest in sharing attention or interacting;
  • there are family members with language or reading difficulties.

Seek a prompt assessment, at any age, if:

  • the child has lost words or skills they already had;
  • they do not react to sounds or voices, or hearing problems are suspected;
  • there are difficulties swallowing or breathing, or sudden changes.

Who should you talk to?

  1. The family doctor or paediatrician, who looks at overall development and refers on.
  2. A hearing test. A normal newborn hearing screen does not rule out hearing problems that appear later.
  3. A speech and language therapist (speech-language pathologist), who assesses understanding, expression, speech sounds and communication, and guides the family.

You do not need a diagnosis to start receiving guidance or support.

Myths that delay help

What we know

  • Talking is not a matter of willpower.
  • Learning two languages does not cause language disorders.
  • A speech delay on its own does not diagnose autism or any other condition.
  • Understanding well does not rule out difficulties with expression or sounds.

Myths

  • «They’ll talk when they’re ready.»
  • «It’s laziness or stubbornness.»
  • «It’s because of two languages at home.»
  • «Wait until they’re 3.»

What you can do at home while you wait

  • comment on what the child is doing, in short sentences;
  • give them time to respond, without asking them to repeat;
  • value gestures, sounds and pointing as real communication;
  • play «my turn, your turn» games;
  • use pictures to help the child choose and ask.

You will find more ideas in Activities to encourage speech. If the child communicates mainly without words, augmentative and alternative communication can help straight away and does not stop speech.

Frequently asked questions about speech delay

Is it normal for a 2-year-old not to talk?

Children vary, but a 2-year-old who uses hardly any words or does not combine words should be assessed. Talking to the family doctor or paediatrician is the first step.

Is it a speech delay or autism?

A speech delay on its own does not mean a child is autistic. Some autistic children talk late, others talk within the expected time, and many children who talk late are not autistic. Only an assessment that also looks at understanding, gestures, shared attention and hearing can clarify it. Losing words or skills the child already had calls for a prompt assessment.

A 2-year-old understands everything but is not talking. Should I worry?

Understanding well is a good sign, but it does not rule out a difficulty with expressing or with speech sounds. If, at around 2, the child still uses very few words or does not combine words, talk to the family doctor or paediatrician. Meanwhile, respond to gestures and pointing as real communication.

What is the difference between a speech delay and a language delay?

A speech delay concerns producing sounds; a language delay concerns vocabulary, sentences and understanding. They can occur separately or together.

Can an app fix a speech delay?

No. An app can be a resource for playing and talking with an adult, but it does not replace an assessment or speech therapy.

Should I wait until age 3?

That is not a safe rule. Assessment and support do not need to wait for a definite diagnosis.

This article is for information only and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you are worried about a child’s development, talk to their doctor or paediatrician.
